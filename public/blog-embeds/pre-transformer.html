The long road to "Attention Is All You Need"

For thirty years, language models fought a single enemy: carrying information across a long sequence. N-grams, RNNs, LSTMs, and encoder–decoders were four escalating answers, each fixing the last one's failure and exposing a new one. Follow that chain and the 2017 transformer stops looking like a bolt from the blue — it looks inevitable. Here is exactly which bottlenecks forced it.

read  ~22 min history  1948 → 2017 interactive  path length · alignment series  Bedrock · 4

Read this sentence and resolve the pronoun: "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big." What is it — the trophy or the suitcase? You know it's the trophy, and to know that you had to connect it back across nine words to trophy. That reach — linking two tokens that sit far apart in a sequence — is the one skill every language model must have, and for three decades it was precisely the thing our models could not do well.

The history of language modeling before 2017 is best read not as a parade of unrelated architectures but as a single problem attacked four times. Each method can be scored on two brutal questions:

  • How far must information travel to connect two related tokens — the path length?
  • How much of that travel is stuck being sequential — done one step at a time, unable to use a GPU's parallelism?
The thesis

Every pre-transformer architecture is a different answer to "how does information cross a long sequence," and each is defined by its path length and its sequential cost. The story marches in one direction — shrinking the path — until attention makes it O(1)O(1)O(1) and fully parallel, at which point the recurrent machinery everyone had been nursing along becomes pure dead weight. That realization is the transformer.

We will build the chain link by link, and you will be able to watch the path length explode for recurrence and collapse for attention on a slider. Let's start where it started: with counting.

1 · N-grams: condition on the last few words, and only those

The earliest and simplest language model, tracing to Shannon's 1948 information theory, makes a blunt approximation. The chain rule says the probability of a token depends on all preceding tokens, P(wt∣w1,…,wt−1)P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1})P(wt​∣w1​,…,wt−1​). That conditional is impossible to estimate directly, so the Markov assumption throws away all but the last k−1k-1k−1 words:

P(wt∣w1,…,wt−1)  ≈  P(wt∣wt−k+1,…,wt−1).P(w_t \mid w_1,\dots,w_{t-1}) \;\approx\; P(w_t \mid w_{t-k+1},\dots,w_{t-1}).P(wt​∣w1​,…,wt−1​)≈P(wt​∣wt−k+1​,…,wt−1​).

A trigram model (k=3k=3k=3) predicts the next word from just the previous two, and estimates the probability by counting: how often did "cat sat" precede each word in a big corpus, normalized. It is fast, transparent, and for decades it was the backbone of speech recognition and machine translation.

But it walks straight into two walls. First, the fixed horizon: a trigram model literally cannot see past two words. In "The trophy didn't fit … because it was too big," the link from it to trophy is simply invisible; beyond the window, the model is blind. Enlarging kkk hits the second wall, sparsity: the number of possible kkk-grams is VkV^kVk, so counts become vanishingly small and most longer contexts are never observed even once. And because words are atomic symbols, the model learns nothing transferable — seeing "black cat" a thousand times tells it nothing about "black dog." A fixed, un-generalizing window: that is the ceiling.

To break the fixed horizon we need a model that can, in principle, carry unbounded history in a running summary. That is a recurrent network.

2 · RNNs: a running summary, and the gradient that dies

A recurrent neural network (Elman, 1990) keeps a hidden state hth_tht​, a fixed-size vector it updates at every token by mixing the new input with the previous state:

ht  =  tanh⁡ ⁣(Wh ht−1+Wx xt+b),yt  =  softmax(Wo ht).h_t \;=\; \tanh\!\big(W_h\, h_{t-1} + W_x\, x_t + b\big), \qquad y_t \;=\; \mathrm{softmax}(W_o\, h_t).ht​=tanh(Wh​ht−1​+Wx​xt​+b),yt​=softmax(Wo​ht​).

The elegance is that hth_tht​ is a function of ht−1h_{t-1}ht−1​, which folds in ht−2h_{t-2}ht−2​, and so on back to the start — so in principle the state summarizes the entire history, with no fixed window. The trophy could inform the pronoun a hundred words later. That is a real conceptual leap past n-grams.

In practice it fails, for a reason that is pure calculus. To learn a dependency across kkk steps, gradients must flow backward through kkk repeated multiplications by the same weight matrix. The sensitivity of a later state to an earlier one is a product of Jacobians,

∂ht∂ht−k  =  ∏i=1k∂ht−i+1∂ht−i,\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-k}} \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{k} \frac{\partial h_{t-i+1}}{\partial h_{t-i}},∂ht−k​∂ht​​=i=1∏k​∂ht−i​∂ht−i+1​​,

and a product of kkk matrices shrinks toward zero if their gain is below one and blows up if it is above one. This is the vanishing/exploding gradient problem (Hochreiter 1991; Bengio et al. 1994): the learning signal for long-range dependencies decays exponentially with distance. The RNN can represent unbounded history, but it cannot learn to use it. And note the second cost, which will matter enormously later: computing hth_tht​ requires ht−1h_{t-1}ht−1​ first, so the nnn steps are inherently sequential — a path length of O(n)O(n)O(n) and O(n)O(n)O(n) non-parallelizable operations.

If the problem is a signal decaying as it is repeatedly squashed and re-multiplied, the fix is a memory highway that information can ride along without being squashed at every step.

3 · LSTMs: a memory conveyor belt with gates

The Long Short-Term Memory network (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997) adds a second state, the cell state ctc_tct​, which Christopher Olah memorably describes as a conveyor belt: information can ride it across many steps almost unchanged. Crucially, the cell is updated additively, guarded by learned gates — sigmoid valves that decide what to erase, what to write, and what to read:

ft=σ(Wf[ht−1,xt]),it=σ(Wi[ht−1,xt]),ot=σ(Wo[ht−1,xt]),f_t = \sigma(W_f[h_{t-1},x_t]), \quad i_t = \sigma(W_i[h_{t-1},x_t]), \quad o_t = \sigma(W_o[h_{t-1},x_t]),ft​=σ(Wf​[ht−1​,xt​]),it​=σ(Wi​[ht−1​,xt​]),ot​=σ(Wo​[ht−1​,xt​]),
ct  =  ft⊙ct−1⏟keep  +  it⊙c~t⏟write,ht  =  ot⊙tanh⁡(ct).c_t \;=\; \underbrace{f_t \odot c_{t-1}}_{\text{keep}} \;+\; \underbrace{i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t}_{\text{write}}, \qquad h_t \;=\; o_t \odot \tanh(c_t).ct​=keepft​⊙ct−1​​​+writeit​⊙c~t​​​,ht​=ot​⊙tanh(ct​).

The forget gate ftf_tft​ decides how much of the old memory to retain; the input gate iti_tit​ how much new candidate content c~t\tilde{c}_tc~t​ to add; the output gate oto_tot​ what to expose as the hidden state. The magic is the additive path ct=ft⊙ct−1+…c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + \dotsct​=ft​⊙ct−1​+…: when the forget gate stays near 1, the gradient flows back through the cell with a gain near 1 instead of being multiplied down, so it neither vanishes nor explodes nearly as fast. LSTMs (and the lighter GRU) pushed usable dependency lengths from a handful of words to dozens or hundreds, and powered a decade of sequence modeling.

Progress, but the same shape of problem An LSTM lengthens the range before the signal fades — it does not abolish the fade, and it does not touch the sequential cost. Connecting token 1 to token 100 still means marching the state through 99 gated updates, one after another. Path length and sequential operations are both still O(n)O(n)O(n). The memory got better; the road stayed just as long.

So far we have modeled one sequence. Translation needs two — read a whole source sentence, then write a whole target sentence. That framing exposes the sharpest bottleneck yet.

4 · Seq2seq: squeeze the whole sentence through one vector

The sequence-to-sequence model (Sutskever, Vinyals & Le, 2014; Cho et al., 2014) put two RNNs back to back. An encoder LSTM reads the entire source sentence and compresses it into its final hidden state — a single fixed-length vector ccc. A decoder LSTM is then initialized from ccc and generates the translation one token at a time:

c=hTenc,P(yi∣y<i,x)=decoder(yi−1,si,c).c = h^{\text{enc}}_{T}, \qquad P(y_i \mid y_{<i}, x) = \text{decoder}\big(y_{i-1}, s_i, c\big).c=hTenc​,P(yi​∣y<i​,x)=decoder(yi−1​,si​,c).

This worked, startlingly well: Sutskever's 4-layer LSTM reached 34.8 BLEU on WMT'14 English→French, edging past the mature phrase-based statistical system's 33.3 — the first time a pure neural model won at scale. Deep learning had arrived in machine translation.

But stare at that single vector ccc. Everything about the source sentence — a 40-word legal clause, all of its nouns and clauses and their order — must be crushed into one fixed-size vector, and the decoder sees nothing else. This is the fixed-length context bottleneck, and its fingerprints were everywhere. Translation quality fell off a cliff as sentences grew longer. And the era's most telling hack: Sutskever found that reversing the source sentence — feeding it in backwards — lifted BLEU from 25.9 to 30.6, purely because it placed the first few source words nearer their translations and shortened those particular paths. When reversing your input is a top result, the path length is screaming for attention.

The fix is almost obvious in hindsight: stop forcing everything through one vector. Let the decoder look back at the entire source, and learn where to look.

5 · Bahdanau attention: a direct line to every source word

Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio (2014) removed the bottleneck with an idea that would, three years later, eat the whole field. Instead of one fixed ccc, compute a fresh context vector cic_ici​ for every output step iii, as a weighted sum of all encoder states:

ci  =  ∑j=1Tαij hj,αij  =  exp⁡(eij)∑j′exp⁡(eij′),eij=a(si−1,hj).c_i \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{T} \alpha_{ij}\, h_j, \qquad \alpha_{ij} \;=\; \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{j'} \exp(e_{ij'})}, \qquad e_{ij} = a(s_{i-1}, h_j).ci​=j=1∑T​αij​hj​,αij​=∑j′​exp(eij′​)exp(eij​)​,eij​=a(si−1​,hj​).

The alignment weights αij\alpha_{ij}αij​ are a softmax over scores eije_{ij}eij​ that measure how well the decoder's current state si−1s_{i-1}si−1​ matches each source position hjh_jhj​. When generating the French word for "Area," the model can put most of its weight on the English "Area" no matter where it sits. The whole source stays available; the decoder builds a direct, length-1 path to any source token it needs, and it learns the alignment end-to-end without ever being told which words correspond.

Attention solved the bottleneck. But it had been bolted onto a recurrent network — and that host was now the thing holding everything back.

6 · The bottlenecks that remained — and the 2017 leap

By 2016 the state of the art was an RNN encoder–decoder with attention. Attention had given the decoder O(1)O(1)O(1) access to the source. But the recurrent backbone underneath was still imposing two costs that attention did nothing to remove:

  • It is inherently sequential. The RNN must compute h1h_1h1​ before h2h_2h2​ before h3h_3h3​; the nnn steps cannot be done at once. In the words of the 2017 paper, this "inherently sequential nature precludes parallelization within training examples" — catastrophic on GPUs built for parallel matrix math, and worse as sequences grow.
  • Encoder self-dependencies still travel O(n)O(n)O(n). Attention shortened the decoder-to-encoder path, but for one source word to influence another within the encoder, the signal still had to crawl through the recurrence.

Vaswani et al. (2017) asked the question that names the paper: if attention is doing the real work of connecting distant positions, why keep the recurrence at all? Replace it entirely with self-attention — let every position attend directly to every other position in the same sequence — and the costs collapse. Self-attention connects any two positions in a single step (path length O(1)O(1)O(1)) and, because those connections are independent, computes them all at once (sequential ops O(1)O(1)O(1)). The catch is right there in column two: O(n2⋅d)O(n^2 \cdot d)O(n2⋅d) compute per layer, since every position attends to every other. The transformer traded a sequential bottleneck for a quadratic one — a trade that was a bargain in 2017 and became the central problem of the years since. Feel the trade on the slider:

The payoff was immediate and decisive. The big Transformer set a new state of the art of 28.4 BLEU on WMT'14 English→German and 41.8 on English→French, and — the part that changed everything — it did so after just 3.5 days on eight GPUs, a small fraction of the training cost of the recurrent models it beat, precisely because it could finally parallelize across sequence positions.

Why the chain had to end here

Line up the four links and the direction is unmistakable. Each method shortened the path or lengthened the reach, and each exposed the next bottleneck:

Read top to bottom, "Attention Is All You Need" is not a surprise — it is the fixed point the whole sequence was converging on. Once attention delivered O(1)O(1)O(1) paths, the recurrent scaffolding that had carried the field since 1990 had nothing left to contribute but its sequential cost, so they removed it. The title is a literal engineering claim: the recurrence was doing no essential work that attention was not already doing better.

Where this goes next

Everything after 2017 lives inside that last row of the table. The transformer bought O(1)O(1)O(1) path length and full parallelism at the price of O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) attention, and the modern era is largely the campaign to keep the former while shrinking the latter — which is the story of KV caching, multi-query and grouped-query attention, FlashAttention, sliding-window and linear attention, and the state-space revival (Mamba) that, in a twist, brings a form of recurrence back to beat the quadratic cost.

Mechanically, the immediate next step is to open up the self-attention box itself: the queries, keys, and values, the scaled dot-product, and the multi-head trick — the machinery this post has been treating as a black box that gives you O(1)O(1)O(1) paths. The next-token objective, the tokens, and the embeddings from the earlier Bedrock posts are the inputs to that machine. From here on, it is transformers all the way down.
