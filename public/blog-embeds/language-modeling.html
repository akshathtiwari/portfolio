Language modeling is just measuring surprise

Chain rule, autoregressive factorization, cross-entropy, and perplexity are not four separate ideas. They are one idea — score how surprised the model is by the true next token — read four ways. Here it is from scratch, with a worked example where every number checks out.

read  ~18 min math  full derivation interactive  surprise ledger series  Bedrock · 1

Ask a large language model to continue the cat sat on the and it answers mat. Whatever else it is doing, at bottom it is doing exactly one thing, over and over: given the text so far, it produces a probability distribution over what the next token could be. That is the whole job. "Language model" is a precise technical claim — it is a model of the probability of language.

The one idea

A language model assigns a probability to the next token. Everything else in this post — the chain rule, the loss it is trained with, the perplexity it is scored on — is a mechanical consequence of one decision: measure how surprised the model is by the token that actually came next.

We will build a single instrument, a surprise meter, and then watch the same reading come out as three different famous quantities depending on whether we sum it, average it, or exponentiate it. By the end you should be able to derive the training loss of every GPT-style model on a napkin, and know exactly what a perplexity of 16.4 is telling you.

The naive attempt: model the whole sentence at once

Suppose we want the probability of a specific sentence w1w2…wNw_1 w_2 \dots w_Nw1​w2​…wN​ — say, so we can rank candidate transcriptions of noisy speech, or check which of two continuations is more likely. The obvious move: treat the sentence as a single object and estimate its probability directly, P(w1,w2,…,wN)P(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_N)P(w1​,w2​,…,wN​), from how often it appears.

This dies immediately. With a vocabulary of VVV tokens, the number of possible length-NNN sequences is VNV^NVN. For a modest V=50,000V = 50{,}000V=50,000 and a short N=20N = 20N=20, that is

VN=50,000 20≈1094V^N = 50{,}000^{\,20} \approx 10^{94}VN=50,00020≈1094

possible sentences — more than the number of atoms in the observable universe. You will never see the overwhelming majority of them even once, so you can never estimate their probabilities by counting. A table indexed by whole sentences is not big data, it is an impossible object. We need to buy the joint probability without ever building that table.

The escape is not an approximation. It is an exact rewrite of the joint probability that turns one impossible question into many easy ones.

The chain rule: an exact factorization

The chain rule of probability says that any joint distribution factors into a product of conditionals, with no assumptions and no loss:

P(w1,w2,…,wN)  =  ∏t=1NP ⁣(wt∣w1,…,wt−1).P(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_N) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{N} P\!\left(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}\right).P(w1​,w2​,…,wN​)=t=1∏N​P(wt​∣w1​,…,wt−1​).

Read it left to right. The probability of the whole sentence is the probability of the first token, times the probability of the second given the first, times the probability of the third given the first two, and so on. Writing w<tw_{<t}w<t​ for "all tokens before position ttt", this is the compact autoregressive factorization:

P(w1:N)  =  ∏t=1NP ⁣(wt∣w<t).P(w_{1:N}) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{N} P\!\left(w_t \mid w_{<t}\right).P(w1:N​)=t=1∏N​P(wt​∣w<t​).

This is worth pausing on, because the entire field rests on it. We did not approximate anything — this is an identity, true for every distribution over sequences. But it has quietly changed the problem. Instead of one impossible VNV^NVN-way question ("how likely is this exact sentence?"), we now have NNN merely-hard VVV-way questions ("given what came before, how likely is each possible next token?"). Each factor is a distribution over a single vocabulary slot. That is a problem we can actually train a model to answer.

A word on order and "bidirectional" models Left-to-right is a choice, not a law — the chain rule works in any fixed order. GPT-style models pick left-to-right because it matches generation: to produce text you must commit to token ttt before you can condition token t+1t+1t+1 on it. Masked models like BERT instead corrupt-and-restore tokens and do not define a proper autoregressive probability over sequences, which is why perplexity, defined below, is not well-posed for them.

So the model only ever has to answer one kind of question: a distribution over the next token. Let us give that distribution a shape.

The model is a next-token classifier

At each position the model reads the left context w<tw_{<t}w<t​, crunches it (through a transformer, an RNN, whatever — the architecture is irrelevant to this post) into a vector of scores called logits z∈RVz \in \mathbb{R}^{V}z∈RV, one score per vocabulary entry. To turn arbitrary real scores into a probability distribution, we pass them through the softmax:

pθ(wt=v∣w<t)  =  e zv∑u=1Ve zu,v∈{1,…,V}.p_\theta(w_t = v \mid w_{<t}) \;=\; \frac{e^{\,z_v}}{\sum_{u=1}^{V} e^{\,z_u}}, \qquad v \in \{1,\dots,V\}.pθ​(wt​=v∣w<t​)=∑u=1V​ezu​ezv​​,v∈{1,…,V}.

Here θ\thetaθ is all the model's parameters, zvz_vzv​ is the logit for candidate token vvv, and the denominator — the sum over the whole vocabulary — is what forces the outputs to be non-negative and sum to 1. So the model is a giant VVV-way classifier applied once per position, sharing parameters across positions. Nothing more exotic than that.

That is the entire forward story. The interesting question is how we teach θ\thetaθ to put its probability mass in the right place. For that we need a way to score a single prediction.

Surprisal: the reading on the meter

Reality reveals the true next token. The model had assigned it some probability ppp. How badly did the model do? The right currency is not "was it right" — the model outputs a distribution, not a guess — but how surprised it should be. Information theory gives the unique sensible answer, the surprisal:

surprisal(p)  =  −log⁡p.\text{surprisal}(p) \;=\; -\log p.surprisal(p)=−logp.

This is the meter. It has exactly the properties surprise should have. If the model was certain and correct (p=1p = 1p=1), the surprisal is −log⁡1=0-\log 1 = 0−log1=0 — no surprise, no penalty. As the probability it assigned to the truth shrinks toward zero, the surprisal climbs without bound. And surprisals add: the surprise of two independent events is the sum of their surprises, which is exactly the additive behavior we want when we start stringing tokens together.

The base of the logarithm just sets the unit. Base 2 gives bits (Shannon's unit — one bit is the surprise of a fair coin landing the way you didn't expect); natural log gives nats, which is what deep-learning code uses because log⁡\loglog and exp⁡\expexp are cheaper. They differ only by a constant factor, 1 nat=log⁡2e≈1.4427 bits1\ \text{nat} = \log_2 e \approx 1.4427\ \text{bits}1 nat=log2​e≈1.4427 bits.

Now we have a reading for one token. To train and to evaluate, we do the two obvious things with a stream of readings: add them, and average them.

Adding the readings: the loss falls out

Take the logarithm of the chain-rule factorization. Products of probabilities become sums of log-probabilities — which is the entire reason we work in log-space (a product of thousands of numbers below 1 would underflow to zero in floating point; a sum of their logs will not):

log⁡P(w1:N)  =  ∑t=1Nlog⁡pθ(wt∣w<t).\log P(w_{1:N}) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{N} \log p_\theta(w_t \mid w_{<t}).logP(w1:N​)=t=1∑N​logpθ​(wt​∣w<t​).

Negate both sides and you have the negative log-likelihood of the sequence — which, term by term, is exactly a sum of surprisals:

−log⁡P(w1:N)⏟NLL of the sentence  =  ∑t=1N(−log⁡pθ(wt∣w<t))⏟surprisal at step t.\underbrace{-\log P(w_{1:N})}_{\text{NLL of the sentence}} \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{N} \underbrace{\big(-\log p_\theta(w_t \mid w_{<t})\big)}_{\text{surprisal at step } t}.NLL of the sentence−logP(w1:N​)​​=t=1∑N​surprisal at step t(−logpθ​(wt​∣w<t​))​​.

The total surprise of a sentence is the sum of the per-token surprises. That is the metaphor made literal. Now, why is this the thing to minimize? Because of maximum likelihood. We want parameters θ\thetaθ that make the observed training data as probable as possible. Maximizing a probability is the same as maximizing its log (log is monotonic), which is the same as minimizing its negative. So "make the data likely" and "minimize summed surprisal" are the same instruction:

θ⋆  =  arg⁡max⁡θ∏iPθ(datai)  =  arg⁡min⁡θ∑i(−log⁡Pθ(datai)).\theta^\star \;=\; \arg\max_\theta \prod_i P_\theta(\text{data}_i) \;=\; \arg\min_\theta \sum_i \big(-\log P_\theta(\text{data}_i)\big).θ⋆=argθmax​i∏​Pθ​(datai​)=argθmin​i∑​(−logPθ​(datai​)).

Why this is also called "cross-entropy"

Here is the identity that confuses everyone the first time. The true next token is a single known symbol — a "one-hot" target: a distribution yyy that puts probability 1 on the actual token ccc and 0 on all others. The cross-entropy between the target distribution yyy and the model's predicted distribution pθp_\thetapθ​ is

H(y,pθ)  =  −∑v=1Vyvlog⁡pθ(v)  =  −log⁡pθ(c),H(y, p_\theta) \;=\; -\sum_{v=1}^{V} y_v \log p_\theta(v) \;=\; -\log p_\theta(c),H(y,pθ​)=−v=1∑V​yv​logpθ​(v)=−logpθ​(c),

because every term in the sum is multiplied by yvy_vyv​, which is zero except at the true token v=cv = cv=c, where it is one. Every other candidate vanishes. So the cross-entropy loss and the negative log-likelihood are the same number whenever the target is a single correct token — which for next-token prediction it always is. "Cross-entropy loss", "negative log-likelihood", and "summed surprisal" are three names for one quantity. When people say a model is "trained with cross-entropy", this is all they mean: at each position, penalize it by the surprisal of the token that actually came next.

Summed surprisal grows with sentence length, so it cannot compare a model across texts of different sizes. The fix is the second obvious operation: divide by the number of tokens.

Averaging the readings: cross-entropy per token

Divide the summed surprisal by the number of predicted tokens and you get the model's average cross-entropy, the per-token loss that training actually reports and minimizes:

L(θ)  =  −1N∑t=1Nlog⁡pθ(wt∣w<t)[nats/token].\mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} \log p_\theta(w_t \mid w_{<t}) \quad\text{[nats/token]}.L(θ)=−N1​t=1∑N​logpθ​(wt​∣w<t​)[nats/token].

This is a per-token average surprise: on a held-out corpus, how many nats of surprise does the model suffer, on average, each time it has to predict the next token. Lower is better, and it is comparable across corpora of any length because the length has been divided out.

What minimizing it really does

There is a clean information-theoretic reading. If the real world emits tokens from some true distribution P⋆P^\starP⋆ and the model believes pθp_\thetapθ​, the expected cross-entropy decomposes exactly as

H(P⋆,pθ)  =  H(P⋆)⏟the language’s own entropy  +  DKL ⁣(P⋆ ∥ pθ)⏟how wrong the model is ≥ 0.H(P^\star, p_\theta) \;=\; \underbrace{H(P^\star)}_{\text{the language's own entropy}} \;+\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P^\star \,\|\, p_\theta\right)}_{\text{how wrong the model is}\,\ge\,0}.H(P⋆,pθ​)=the language’s own entropyH(P⋆)​​+how wrong the model is≥0DKL​(P⋆∥pθ​)​​.

The first term, the intrinsic unpredictability of the language, is a constant we cannot change. The second, the Kullback–Leibler divergence, is ≥0\ge 0≥0 and is zero only when pθ=P⋆p_\theta = P^\starpθ​=P⋆. So minimizing cross-entropy is literally minimizing the distance between the model and the true distribution of the language. The loss has a floor — the language's own entropy — that no model, however large, can beat. That floor is why perplexity does not go to 1.

A per-token surprise in nats is correct but hard to feel. Exponentiating it turns it back into something you can picture: a number of choices.

Perplexity: surprise as an effective menu size

Exponentiate the average cross-entropy — undoing the logarithm that defined surprisal — and you get perplexity:

PPL  =  exp⁡ ⁣(L(θ))  =  exp⁡ ⁣(−1N∑t=1Nlog⁡pθ(wt∣w<t)).\mathrm{PPL} \;=\; \exp\!\Big(\mathcal{L}(\theta)\Big) \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\log p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{<t})\right).PPL=exp(L(θ))=exp(−N1​t=1∑N​logpθ​(wt​∣w<t​)).

(Use eee with nats, or 2 with bits — same number, since the base you exponentiate must match the base of the log.) Equivalently, perplexity is the inverse geometric mean of the per-token probabilities, PPL=(∏tpt)−1/N\mathrm{PPL} = \big(\prod_t p_t\big)^{-1/N}PPL=(∏t​pt​)−1/N. But the reason it is the metric is its interpretation as an effective branching factor.

Imagine a model that, at every step, is honestly torn between kkk equally likely tokens and assigns each probability 1/k1/k1/k. Its per-token surprisal is −log⁡(1/k)=log⁡k-\log(1/k) = \log k−log(1/k)=logk, so its cross-entropy is log⁡k\log klogk and its perplexity is exp⁡(log⁡k)=k\exp(\log k) = kexp(logk)=k. So:

How to read a perplexity number

A perplexity of k means the model is, on average, as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among k equally likely tokens at each step. Perplexity 1 = perfect clairvoyance. Perplexity = vocabulary size VVV = the model learned nothing (uniform guessing). Real models live in between, and lower is better.

This gives you two anchor points to sanity-check any perplexity you read. The ceiling: a model that ignores the input and guesses uniformly over a 50k vocabulary has perplexity 50,000. The floor: a model that has perfectly captured the language sits at exp⁡(H(P⋆))\exp(H(P^\star))exp(H(P⋆)), the entropy of the language itself — for English, estimated at roughly 1 bit per character, nowhere near 1. GPT-2's ~16–20 (below) means: faced with the next token, it behaves as though hesitating among ~16–20 options rather than 50,000. That collapse from 50,000 to ~18 is what "the model learned English" looks like as a single number.

Two things are worth extracting. First, the collapse: a uniform model over GPT-2's ~50,257-token vocabulary would score a perplexity of 50,257; the trained model scores ~16–20. That factor of ~2,500 is the learning. Second, look at how much the number moves — from 19.44 to 16.44 — without touching the model at all. The only change is giving each prediction more left context by sliding the window instead of chopping the text into disjoint blocks. A fixed-context model literally cannot condition on tokens beyond its window, so the naive disjoint-chunk method starves most predictions of context and reports a worse (higher) perplexity than the model deserves. Perplexity measures the model and the evaluation protocol together.

What perplexity does not tell you

The metric is exact, but it is narrow. Naming its limits is what makes it trustworthy.

  • It is tokenizer-dependent, so cross-model comparisons can be meaningless. Perplexity is per-token, and two models with different tokenizers cut the same text into different numbers of tokens. A model with a bigger vocabulary spreads the same sentence over fewer, "easier" steps and can post a lower perplexity while being no better. You can only compare perplexities computed over the identical tokenization. (This is the through-line to the companion post on tokenization.)
  • Low perplexity is not helpfulness. It measures held-out likelihood — how unsurprised the model is by real text — not whether the model is truthful, harmless, or useful. Instruction-tuning and RLHF often raise perplexity on raw web text while making a model far more useful. Perplexity watches pre-training work; it does not measure the qualities we actually deploy for.
  • Training uses teacher forcing; generation does not. Every conditional above is scored on the true prefix w<tw_{<t}w<t​. At generation time the model instead conditions on its own previous outputs, so a single early mistake shifts it onto a prefix it was never scored on. Low teacher-forced perplexity does not guarantee good free-running generation — the "exposure" gap.
  • The average hides its own tail. A per-token mean can look healthy while a handful of catastrophic tokens (rare names, code, numbers) dominate the sum — exactly what the "catastrophic token" preset in the widget demonstrates.

Where this goes next

You now have the objective that every GPT-style model is trained and evaluated on, derived from a single decision. It is worth seeing how completely it recurs: the same per-token cross-entropy is the pre-training loss, the fine-tuning loss, and (exponentiated) the evaluation metric. Nearly everything else in modern LLMs is an attempt to either lower this number (better architectures, more data, more parameters) or to stop optimizing it once it stops correlating with usefulness (the pivot to RLHF).

Two threads lead directly out of here. One is tokenization — the choice of what a "token" even is, which silently sets the units of everything above and, as the companion post shows, is behind a surprising number of weird model failures. The other is decoding: this post scored a given sequence, but generating one means sampling from these distributions (temperature, top-kkk, nucleus), where the same softmax you met here reappears with a knob on it.
