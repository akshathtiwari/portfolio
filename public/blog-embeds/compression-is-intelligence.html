Compression Is Intelligence

There is a startling claim floating around AI: that compressing text well and understanding it are the same problem. This is not a metaphor. There is a precise chain of mathematics — from a robot on the moon to the loss function of every large language model — that makes prediction and compression two names for one thing. Let us rebuild that chain from scratch.

A ground-up derivation of self-information, entropy, and cross-entropy, and why cross_entropy_loss is secretly a compression objective. Inspired by 3Blue1Brown's Reinventing Entropy; grounded in Shannon (1951) and DeepMind's Language Modeling Is Compression (2023).

01The provocation

Marcus Hutter, an AI researcher, has for years offered a cash prize for one very specific feat: compress a fixed 1 gigabyte snapshot of English Wikipedia (a file called enwik9) smaller than anyone before you. The fund stands at €500,000, paying out €5,000 for every 1% you shave off the record. It is called the Hutter Prize, and its stated purpose is not better zip files. It is artificial general intelligence.

The reasoning is a syllogism worth taking seriously. To compress text, you must predict it: a compressor earns its savings by being unsurprised, by knowing what is likely to come next. And to predict Wikipedia — to know that "the capital of France is" is almost always followed by "Paris," that a sentence about mitochondria will use the word "energy" — you need a working model of the world. Compress well enough and you must, somewhere inside, understand.

"Being able to compress well is closely related to acting intelligently." — the premise of the Hutter Prize.

The phrase compression is intelligence is provocative and, because "intelligence" resists definition, impossible to prove. But strip away the philosophy and a hard mathematical fact remains, one that quietly runs every language model on Earth. To see it, we start not with intelligence but with a much smaller question: what is the smallest number of bits it takes to send a message?

Where we are going: a single quantity, −log₂(p), will turn out to answer that question — and it is the same quantity sitting inside the loss function of GPT.

02The naive code, and why it wastes

Imagine you are controlling a rover on the Moon, and the only thing you ever send it is one of four driving instructions: up, down, left, right. Bandwidth to the Moon is precious, so every bit counts.

The obvious encoding gives each instruction a fixed two-bit codeword: up=00, down=01, left=10, right=11. Four messages, two bits each, done. This is exactly how ASCII treats text — every character, common or rare, gets the same 8 bits. It is simple, and it is wasteful, for one reason: it pretends every instruction is equally likely.

They are not. Suppose the rover mostly drives forward, and telemetry shows the instructions occur with these probabilities:

P(up)=12,P(down)=14,P(left)=18,P(right)=18.P(\text{up})=\tfrac12,\quad P(\text{down})=\tfrac14,\quad P(\text{left})=\tfrac18,\quad P(\text{right})=\tfrac18.P(up)=21​,P(down)=41​,P(left)=81​,P(right)=81​.

Half the messages you ever send are up. Spending two full bits on the thing you say half the time is like giving your most common word the longest name. The fix, known since the 1950s, is a variable-length code: short codewords for frequent symbols, long ones for rare symbols. But you cannot just hand out short codes freely — there is a budget, and the first widget lets you feel it.

Notice what the widget forces on you. Make one codeword shorter and you must make another longer; the budget ∑ 2−ℓ ≤ 1 is conserved. So the question "what is the best code?" becomes a constrained optimisation: minimise the average length subject to the budget. That problem has a clean answer, and it is the hinge of everything that follows.

03The one formula: information is −log₂(p)

Let symbol i have probability pi and codeword length ℓi. We want to minimise the expected length while spending no more than the whole budget:

min⁡ℓ ∑ipi ℓisubject to∑i2−ℓi≤1.\min_{\ell}\ \sum_i p_i\,\ell_i \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_i 2^{-\ell_i}\le 1.ℓmin​ i∑​pi​ℓi​subject toi∑​2−ℓi​≤1.

Treat the lengths as real numbers for a moment and use a Lagrange multiplier. Setting the derivative of ∑ipiℓi+λ(∑i2−ℓi−1)\sum_i p_i\ell_i + \lambda\big(\sum_i 2^{-\ell_i}-1\big)∑i​pi​ℓi​+λ(∑i​2−ℓi​−1) to zero gives pi=λln⁡2⋅2−ℓip_i = \lambda\ln 2\cdot 2^{-\ell_i}pi​=λln2⋅2−ℓi​, and forcing the budget to be exactly full (∑i2−ℓi=1\sum_i 2^{-\ell_i}=1∑i​2−ℓi​=1) pins the constant so that

 ℓi=−log⁡2pi \boxed{\ \ell_i = -\log_2 p_i\ } ℓi​=−log2​pi​ ​

This is self-information (also called surprisal): the ideal number of bits to spend on an outcome of probability ppp is −log⁡2p-\log_2 p−log2​p. It is worth pausing on how sensible this is:

  • An outcome you were certain of (p=1p=1p=1) carries −log⁡21=0-\log_2 1 = 0−log2​1=0 bits. Telling someone a thing they already knew communicates nothing.
  • A coin-flip (p=12p=\tfrac12p=21​) carries exactly 111 bit — the definition of a bit.
  • A one-in-a-thousand event (p=11024p=\tfrac{1}{1024}p=10241​) carries 101010 bits. The rarer the event, the more it tells you when it happens.

The clean intuition, and the reason the logarithm appears: a bit is a halving of possibility. −log⁡2p-\log_2 p−log2​p counts how many times you must halve the space of possibilities to single out an event of probability ppp. Drag the dial and watch surprise grow as probability shrinks.

Why the bits of a message add up

Independent symbols have probabilities that multiply: P(cat)=P(c) P(a) P(t)P(\text{cat}) = P(c)\,P(a)\,P(t)P(cat)=P(c)P(a)P(t). Because the logarithm turns products into sums, their surprisals add:

−log⁡2P(cat)=−log⁡2P(c)−log⁡2P(a)−log⁡2P(t).-\log_2 P(\text{cat}) = -\log_2 P(c) - \log_2 P(a) - \log_2 P(t).−log2​P(cat)=−log2​P(c)−log2​P(a)−log2​P(t).

So the ideal length of a whole message is just the sum of the ideal lengths of its symbols. This additivity is why a per-symbol quantity is enough to reason about compressing arbitrarily long text — and it is exactly why a language model's loss is summed over tokens.

Where we are going: if each symbol costs −log⁡2p-\log_2 p−log2​p bits, the average cost of a symbol from a whole distribution is the weighted sum ∑ipi(−log⁡2pi)\sum_i p_i(-\log_2 p_i)∑i​pi​(−log2​pi​). That average has a name.

04Entropy: the floor you cannot beat

A source that emits symbols with probabilities pip_ipi​, encoded optimally, spends on average

H(p)  =  ∑ipi (−log⁡2pi)  =  −∑ipilog⁡2pibits per symbol.H(p) \;=\; \sum_i p_i\,\big(-\log_2 p_i\big) \;=\; -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i \quad\text{bits per symbol.}H(p)=i∑​pi​(−log2​pi​)=−i∑​pi​log2​pi​bits per symbol.

This is Shannon entropy. Legend has it von Neumann told Shannon to call it that, half because it matched a formula in statistical mechanics and half because "nobody knows what entropy really is, so in a debate you'll always have the advantage." Its meaning here is concrete: entropy is the average number of bits an optimal code spends per symbol — the hard floor on compression. Shannon's source-coding theorem makes this exact: no lossless code can beat H(p)H(p)H(p) bits per symbol on average, and codes exist that get arbitrarily close.

For the rover, H=12(1)+14(2)+18(3)+18(3)=1.75H = \tfrac12(1) + \tfrac14(2) + \tfrac18(3) + \tfrac18(3) = 1.75H=21​(1)+41​(2)+81​(3)+81​(3)=1.75 bits — the very floor the first widget dared you to reach. Now make the distribution yourself and watch the floor move.

05From integers to fractions: arithmetic coding

There is a crack in the story so far. Self-information says up (with p=12p=\tfrac12p=21​) deserves −log⁡212=1-\log_2\tfrac12 = 1−log2​21​=1 bit and that works cleanly. But what about a symbol with p=13p = \tfrac13p=31​? Its ideal length is −log⁡213≈1.585-\log_2\tfrac13 \approx 1.585−log2​31​≈1.585 bits, and you cannot emit 1.585 of a bit. The classic fix, Huffman coding, rounds each length up to a whole number; it is provably within 1 bit of the entropy per symbol, but that rounding is pure waste when probabilities are not neat powers of two.

Arithmetic coding removes the rounding entirely by refusing to give each symbol its own codeword at all. Instead it encodes the whole message as a single number in the interval [0,1)[0,1)[0,1). Start with the full interval. Each symbol narrows it to the sub-slice whose width equals that symbol's probability; the next symbol subdivides that slice the same way; and so on. The final message occupies a tiny sub-interval whose width is the product of all the symbol probabilities. To transmit the message you send just enough bits to name a point inside that final interval — and naming a point in an interval of width www takes about −log⁡2w-\log_2 w−log2​w bits.

This is the whole trick. Because the final width is w=∏tp(xt)w=\prod_t p(x_t)w=∏t​p(xt​), the code length is −log⁡2∏tp(xt)=∑t−log⁡2p(xt)-\log_2\prod_t p(x_t) = \sum_t -\log_2 p(x_t)−log2​∏t​p(xt​)=∑t​−log2​p(xt​) — the sum of surprisals, with no per-symbol rounding. Arithmetic coding gets within about 2 bits of the entropy for the entire message, not per symbol, so the overhead vanishes as the message grows. Fractional bits, finally paid.

The width is the product of the letters' probabilities; the bits are its negative log. This is exactly how a language model's per-token probabilities become an actual, decodable compressed file.

06The catch: you never know the true p

Everything above assumes you know the real probabilities ppp. But nobody hands you the true distribution of English, or of Wikipedia, or of the rover's commands. You only ever have a model — a guess qqq at the distribution. You build your code from qqq, giving each symbol length −log⁡2q(x)-\log_2 q(x)−log2​q(x), and then reality feeds you symbols drawn from the true ppp. How many bits do you actually spend?

On average, coding true-ppp data with a qqq-based code costs

H(p,q)  =  −∑xp(x) log⁡2q(x)bits per symbol.H(p,q) \;=\; -\sum_x p(x)\,\log_2 q(x) \quad\text{bits per symbol.}H(p,q)=−x∑​p(x)log2​q(x)bits per symbol.

This is cross-entropy: the expected cost of encoding ppp with a code built for qqq. It decomposes beautifully. Adding and subtracting the true surprisal log⁡2p(x)\log_2 p(x)log2​p(x) splits it into two terms:

H(p,q)  =  −∑xp(x)log⁡2p(x)⏟H(p) — the floor  +  ∑xp(x)log⁡2p(x)q(x)⏟DKL(p ∥ q) — the tax.H(p,q) \;=\; \underbrace{-\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x)}_{H(p)\ \text{— the floor}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_x p(x)\log_2\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}}_{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\,\|\,q)\ \text{— the tax}}.H(p,q)=H(p) — the floor−x∑​p(x)log2​p(x)​​+DKL​(p∥q) — the taxx∑​p(x)log2​q(x)p(x)​​​.

The first term is the entropy H(p)H(p)H(p) — the irreducible floor, the bits you would spend even with a perfect model. The second term is the Kullback–Leibler divergence DKL(p∥q)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)DKL​(p∥q): the extra bits you waste for every symbol because your model is wrong. It is zero when q=pq=pq=p and strictly positive otherwise (a fact called Gibbs' inequality). So:

H(p,q)⏟what you pay  =  H(p)⏟unavoidable  +  DKL(p∥q)⏟your model’s badness, in bits.\underbrace{H(p,q)}_{\text{what you pay}} \;=\; \underbrace{H(p)}_{\text{unavoidable}} \;+\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)}_{\text{your model's badness, in bits}}.what you payH(p,q)​​=unavoidableH(p)​​+your model’s badness, in bitsDKL​(p∥q)​​.

Cross-entropy is the entropy floor plus a tax, and the tax is exactly how wrong your model is — measured in wasted bits.

Minimising cross-entropy means driving your model toward the truth.

07The reveal: that tax is the LLM loss

Here is where the moon rover becomes GPT. When a language model is pre-trained, the objective — the "one line of code" everyone quotes — is cross-entropy loss on next-token prediction. For a text x1,x2,…,xTx_1, x_2, \dots, x_Tx1​,x2​,…,xT​, the model produces a probability q(xt∣x<t)q(x_t \mid x_{<t})q(xt​∣x<t​) for each token given everything before it, and the loss is

L  =  −1T∑t=1Tlog⁡2q(xt∣x<t).\mathcal{L} \;=\; -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log_2 q(x_t \mid x_{<t}).L=−T1​t=1∑T​log2​q(xt​∣x<t​).

Read that formula again with the last six sections in mind. Each term −log⁡2q(xt∣x<t)-\log_2 q(x_t\mid x_{<t})−log2​q(xt​∣x<t​) is the number of bits arithmetic coding would spend to encode token xtx_txt​ using the model's own predicted distribution. The sum is the total size, in bits, of the compressed text. The average is the cross-entropy between the true language and the model — the bits-per-token the model achieves.

Training a language model to predict the next token is identical to training it to compress its training data as small as possible. Prediction and compression are the same optimisation, seen from two sides.

This equivalence runs both ways, and each direction is useful:

  • Predictor → compressor. Take any next-token model, feed its probabilities to an arithmetic coder, and you have a lossless compressor whose output size is exactly the model's cross-entropy loss times the number of tokens.
  • Compressor → predictor. Any compressor implies a probability model — the shorter the code it would assign to a continuation, the more probable it considers that continuation. A good zip program is, quietly, a (bad) language model.

Shannon already did this, with his wife

In 1951 Shannon needed the entropy of English but had no language model, so he used the best one available: a human. In his famous guessing game, his wife Betty would guess the next letter of a book one at a time; correct guesses were recorded as dashes, wrong ones as the true letter. The stream of guesses is a probability model, and from how often she was right Shannon estimated the entropy of English at roughly 0.6 to 1.3 bits per character given about a hundred characters of context — far below ASCII's 8, and below the ~4 bits you get from letter frequencies alone.

Modern researchers do exactly what Shannon did, only the black-box oracle is now a neural network instead of Betty: probe the model for its probability of each next character, and read off how many bits it would need. The methodology is a straight line from 1951 to today.

08The payoff: a text model that beats PNG at images

If prediction really is compression, then a good enough predictor should out-compress purpose-built compressors — even on data it was never designed for. In 2023 a DeepMind team (Delétang and colleagues) tested exactly this in a paper titled, with no room for ambiguity, Language Modeling Is Compression. They took Chinchilla 70B — a large language model trained on text — and used it as the probability engine behind an arithmetic coder, then pointed it at things that are not text.

  • On ImageNet image patches, the text model compressed them to measured 43.4% of raw size — beating PNG, a dedicated image format, at 58.5%.
  • On LibriSpeech audio, it reached 16.4%, beating FLAC, a dedicated audio codec, at 30.3%.

A model that had, in effect, only ever read books turned out to compress pictures and sound better than the specialists — because "predict the next chunk given the previous chunks" is a universal skill, and a model that has learned the deep statistical structure of one modality carries structure that transfers. Compression is a measurable, single-number proxy for how much structure a model has captured. This is the strongest concrete evidence the "compression is intelligence" slogan has.

The honest asterisk (a real one). That 43.4% counts only the encoded bits, not the 70-billion-parameter model you need to decode them. If you must ship the decompressor with the file — the fair rule for a standalone compressor — the model's own size dwarfs any single image, and PNG wins again. The result is about the model as a shared, already-agreed-upon codebook, not about zipping one file on a desert island.

Compression rewrites the scaling laws

This asterisk is not a footnote; it reshapes the theory. A bigger model is a better predictor and so achieves a smaller encoded size — but its parameters are part of the total description length you must account for. The quantity that actually matters is the encoded data plus the model: a compression-flavoured restatement of Occam's razor and of the Minimum Description Length principle. It implies there is a model size that is optimal for a given amount of data — too small and you predict poorly, too big and the model itself becomes the thing you failed to compress. Scaling laws, viewed through this lens, are a statement about that trade-off.

09Where the slogan frays

The mathematics of "prediction = compression" is airtight; the leap to "compression = intelligence" is not, and it is worth being precise about the seams:

  • The true floor is unknowable. The smallest a file could ever be compressed is its Kolmogorov complexity — the length of the shortest program that outputs it. That quantity is provably uncomputable; no method can find it in general. So we never actually know the real entropy of English, only ever-tightening estimates. Every "we approached the limit" claim is approaching a limit we cannot see.
  • "Intelligence" is doing a lot of work. The theorems are about bits, not minds. That a compressor must capture regularities is true; that capturing regularities is intelligence is a philosophical stance, not a proof. Compression is necessary for prediction, plausibly necessary for intelligence, and demonstrably not sufficient for everything we mean by the word.
  • The codebook has to be shared. As above, the impressive ratios assume both sides already hold the giant model. Prediction-as-compression is a lens on learning, not a practical zip utility — LLM-based decompression is also punishingly slow.
  • Tokenisation muddies the units. "Bits per character" and "bits per token" are not the same, and comparisons across tokenizers or against byte-level codecs need care. The paper itself notes tokenisation acts as a pre-compression step that changes what the model sees.

None of this dulls the core result. It sharpens it: the precise, provable claim is that a language model's cross-entropy loss is literally the compression rate it achieves, and driving that loss down is driving the model toward the true structure of its data. Whether you call the endpoint "intelligence" is, appropriately, up to you.

The one thing to keep: next-token cross-entropy is not an arbitrary training loss borrowed for convenience. It is the number of bits it takes to write the data down using the model's beliefs. Lower loss = smaller file = a model that has found more of the pattern. That is the whole idea, and it is exact.
