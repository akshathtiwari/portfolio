Phase 1 · Attention  /  the first escape

Don't attend to everything. Attend like a rook.

The first serious escape from attention's quadratic cost was not to approximate it or reschedule it, but to skip most of it — cleverly. The Sparse Transformer (2019) lets each token attend to only √n others, arranged so any two tokens still reach each other in two hops. Here is how strided and fixed patterns work, why the magic number is √n, and the exact O(n√n) it buys.

The previous post left us at a wall: attention is O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) because every token attends to every token, and that all-pairs interaction is the whole point. In 2019, Child, Gray, Radford, and Sutskever asked the obvious dangerous question — does every token really need to attend to every other one? — and found the first good answer. Their Sparse Transformer attends to a carefully chosen O(n)O(\sqrt{n})O(n​) positions per token and still keeps the whole sequence connected.

The danger is real: attend to a random subset and you sever the long-range dependencies that made attention worth having. The insight is that you don't need every pair connected directly — you only need a short path between every pair.

The one idea

Full attention connects all n2n^2n2 pairs in one hop. But you can connect all pairs in two hops with far fewer edges: let every token attend to a local window plus a strided set of relay positions, chosen so any token can reach any other by hopping through a shared relay. Pick the stride to be n\sqrt{n}n​ and each token needs only O(n)O(\sqrt{n})O(n​) connections — total cost O(nn)O(n\sqrt{n})O(nn​) instead of O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2), with global reach intact.

The cleanest way to see this is to stop thinking of the sequence as a line and start thinking of it as a grid.

The rook's move: strided attention

Take a sequence of length nnn and mentally fold it into a n×n\sqrt{n} \times \sqrt{n}n​×n​ grid, row by row. Now give each position two cheap attention patterns, run as two factorized attention heads:

  • Local (the row). Attend to the previous ℓ\ellℓ positions — your immediate neighbours, which in the grid is the rest of your row.
  • Strided (the column). Attend to every ℓ\ellℓ-th position — which in the grid is your column.

Formally, for a query at position ttt with stride ℓ\ellℓ, the two attention sets are

Atlocal={ t−1, t−2, …, t−ℓ },Atstride={ j≤t:(t−j) mod ℓ=0 }.A^{\text{local}}_t = \{\,t-1,\, t-2,\, \dots,\, t-\ell\,\}, \qquad A^{\text{stride}}_t = \{\,j \le t : (t-j)\bmod \ell = 0\,\}.Atlocal​={t−1,t−2,…,t−ℓ},Atstride​={j≤t:(t−j)modℓ=0}.

Here is why this keeps everything connected. On a chessboard, a rook can reach any square from any other in at most two moves: slide along the column, then along the row. Strided attention gives every token exactly the rook's reach — a column hop to line up, a row hop to arrive — so any position can attend to any earlier position in at most two layers, even though each individual attention touches only O(n)O(\sqrt{n})O(n​) positions. This pattern shines on data with natural 2-D or periodic structure — images and audio — where the grid's rows and columns line up with real spatial or temporal periods.

Landmarks instead of a grid: fixed attention

The strided pattern assumes the useful structure repeats every ℓ\ellℓ positions. For text, that periodic assumption is arbitrary — token 200 is not specially related to token 200 − ℓ\ellℓ. So the paper offers a second pattern, fixed attention, built for unstructured sequences:

  • Local block. Attend within your current block of ℓ\ellℓ consecutive tokens (recent context).
  • Landmark columns. Attend to a small fixed set of summary positions — the last position of every previous block — which act as relays: each block funnels its information into its landmark, and every later token can read all landmarks.

Instead of the rook's row-and-column, information now flows through a handful of landmark hubs: your block's tokens write into your block's landmark, and future tokens attend to the landmarks to receive a compressed summary of everything before them. Same two-hop principle — token → landmark → token — but the relays are fixed, content-agnostic summary slots rather than a geometric stride. This is what suits language, where no fixed period exists but a "summarize-this-chunk-and-broadcast-it" motif does. Flip the widget above to Fixed to see the landmark columns (in amber) that every query pulls from.

Two patterns, alternating A Sparse Transformer doesn't pick one pattern — it interleaves the two factorized heads across layers (or merges them), so the model gets both the local window and the long-range relays. The general recipe is ppp factorized attention steps such that any position reaches any other within ppp hops; p=2p = 2p=2 (the row-and-column split) is the workhorse.

Why the magic number is √n

Both patterns split each token's attention into two pieces: a local part of size ℓ\ellℓ and a strided/landmark part of size about n/ℓn/\elln/ℓ (there are n/ℓn/\elln/ℓ strides or blocks to reach across the whole sequence). So the per-token cost is

cost per token  =  ℓ⏟local  +  nℓ⏟strided.\text{cost per token} \;=\; \underbrace{\ell}_{\text{local}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{n}{\ell}}_{\text{strided}}.cost per token=localℓ​​+stridedℓn​​​.

Minimize over the stride ℓ\ellℓ: the sum ℓ+n/ℓ\ell + n/\ellℓ+n/ℓ is smallest when the two terms are equal, ℓ=n/ℓ\ell = n/\ellℓ=n/ℓ, i.e. ℓ=n\ell = \sqrt{n}ℓ=n​. At that stride each token attends to 2n2\sqrt{n}2n​ positions, and across all nnn tokens the total is

n⋅2n  =  O ⁣(nn)  =  O ⁣(n3/2).n \cdot 2\sqrt{n} \;=\; O\!\left(n\sqrt{n}\right) \;=\; O\!\left(n^{3/2}\right).n⋅2n​=O(nn​)=O(n3/2).

That is the whole complexity result. It is not O(n)O(n)O(n) — sparse attention did not make attention linear — but the jump from n2n^2n2 to n1.5n^{1.5}n1.5 is enormous at scale: at n=1Mn = 1\text{M}n=1M it is a 1000×1000\times1000× reduction. What it unlocked

The point of the exercise was scale, and it delivered. With sparse attention plus a set of supporting tricks — architectural changes and initialization to train hundreds of layers deep, gradient recomputation to fit the memory, and custom sparse attention kernels — the Sparse Transformer modeled sequences tens of thousands of timesteps long (with the method feasible, the authors argued, to a million or more) on hardware where dense attention would have been hopeless: roughly a 30× jump in the sequence length you could practically model.

And it was not a toy: a single architecture set new state-of-the-art density-modeling results across three very different modalities at once — Enwik8 (text), CIFAR-10 and ImageNet-64 (images) — while also generating coherent raw audio. That breadth was the real message: the same factorized-sparsity idea worked for anything you could serialize into a long sequence, which is exactly why the whole field followed.

What it costs and what replaced it

  • The patterns are hand-designed, not learned. Strided suits periodic/2-D data; fixed suits text. You must know your data's structure in advance and pick the matching pattern (and the stride ℓ\ellℓ) — a rigidity later "learned sparsity" methods (Routing Transformer, Reformer) tried to remove.
  • Two-hop reach is weaker than one-hop. Distant tokens communicate only indirectly, through relays, so a single sparse layer cannot form an arbitrary long-range interaction the way full attention can; you rely on depth and the relay summaries to carry it, which can lose fidelity.
  • It's still super-linear. O(n3/2)O(n^{3/2})O(n3/2) beats n2n^2n2 but loses to the truly O(n)O(n)O(n) methods (Linformer, Performer, linear attention) at extreme length.
  • Then FlashAttention changed the calculus. By making exact full attention fit in memory and run fast (last post), it removed much of the pressure to approximate at moderate lengths — so for years, full attention stayed dominant and sparsity retreated to the very-long-context frontier.

Where this sits in the lineage

The Sparse Transformer was the opening move of the entire "efficient attention" era — the first family in the quadratic-wall taxonomy: attend to less. Its direct descendants extended the idea with more flexible or provably-expressive sparsity — Longformer and BigBird added global tokens (dedicated relays every token can see), sliding-window attention (Mistral) kept just the local part, and routing/LSH methods learned the sparsity from content. The parallel approximate branch (linear attention) and the reschedule branch (FlashAttention) grew up alongside it.

All of them trace back to the same dangerous question answered here — does every token need to attend to every other? — and the same reassuring answer: not directly, only within a couple of hops. The modern frontier, right down to Kimi K3's linear-attention core, is still living inside the design space this paper opened.
