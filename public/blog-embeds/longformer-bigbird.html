Windows are local. A few tokens go global.

A pure sliding window is cheap but cuts the long-range links attention exists for. Longformer and BigBird fix that with a handful of global tokens that attend to — and are attended by — everything, restoring full-graph connectivity in two hops at O(n) cost. BigBird adds random edges and proves the result is as expressive as full attention.

The Sparse Transformer used fixed strided patterns tuned for images. For documents — QA, summarization, long text — the natural structure is different: attention is mostly local (a word cares about its neighbours), plus a few tokens that everything depends on (the question, a classification token). Longformer (Beltagy et al., 2020) and BigBird (Zaheer et al., 2020) build exactly that attention graph, linear in nnn, and pushed transformers from BERT's 512 tokens to 4,096+.

The one idea

Combine three cheap attention types into one sparse pattern: local (each token attends a window of www neighbours), global (a few special tokens attend to all positions and are attended by all), and — for BigBird — random (each token attends rrr random others). The global tokens are relays: any token reaches any other in two hops through a global token. Total edges are O(n(w+g+r))=O(n)O(n(w+g+r)) = O(n)O(n(w+g+r))=O(n), yet the graph stays fully connected.

Local: the sliding window

Give each token a window: it attends only to the www tokens nearest it (roughly w/2w/2w/2 on each side). That is O(nw)O(n w)O(nw) — linear — and, exactly like a stack of convolutions, the receptive field grows with depth: after LLL layers a token indirectly sees about L⋅wL \cdot wL⋅w tokens of context. Longformer also offers dilated windows (gaps between attended positions) to widen the field without adding compute. Local attention captures the bulk of what language needs — most dependencies really are nearby — for a tiny fraction of the cost.

But "grows with depth" is too slow for a token that needs the whole document now — a question token that must see every passage. Depth-limited local reach is the gap.

Global: a few tokens that see everything

Designate a small set of ggg positions as global. A global token attends to every position, and every position attends to it — a full row and full column in the attention matrix. In Longformer these are chosen by the task: the [CLS] token for classification, or the question tokens for QA, so the answer-bearing passage tokens and the question can see each other directly regardless of distance.

Global tokens are relays. Even if tokens iii and jjj are far apart and outside each other's windows, both attend to (and are attended by) a shared global token, so information flows i→global→ji \to \text{global} \to ji→global→j in two hops. Random: BigBird's small-world edges

BigBird adds the third ingredient: each token also attends to rrr random positions. Borrowing from graph theory, a sparse graph of local + a few random edges is a small-world graph — short paths connect every pair with high probability, so random attention cheaply shrinks the number of hops needed for distant tokens to communicate. Window + global + random is BigBird's full recipe.

The theoretical payoff BigBird's authors did more than show it works — they proved that this sparse window+global+random attention is a universal approximator of sequence-to-sequence functions and is Turing complete, i.e. it retains the expressive power of full attention. That mattered: it turned "sparse attention seems fine empirically" into "sparse attention provably loses nothing in principle," at O(n)O(n)O(n) cost.

The wins were concrete: 8× longer inputs (to 4,096 tokens), state-of-the-art on long-document QA and summarization, and — because a genome is just a very long sequence — new results on DNA modeling.

What it costs

  • Global tokens are chosen, not learned. You decide which positions are global (task-specific: [CLS], question tokens). Pick wrong and a token that needed global reach won't have it.
  • Two non-global, non-window tokens still can't attend directly in one layer — they route through a global token or a random edge, which is indirect and depth-dependent.
  • Irregular patterns fight hardware. Random attention especially is awkward on GPUs; BigBird uses block-random and blocked windows to recover efficiency, and these implementations are fiddlier than dense attention.
  • FlashAttention narrowed the need. For moderate lengths, cheap exact attention reduced the pressure to go sparse — but window+global remains a backbone of long-context and encoder models.

Where this sits in the lineage

Longformer and BigBird refine the sparse family with an explicit global-relay mechanism and (for BigBird) a theory of why it suffices. Their local half lives on directly in sliding-window attention (Mistral, a later post), and the global-token idea keeps resurfacing — as attention sinks, register tokens, and the "a few positions everyone can see" pattern that recurs across long-context designs. The other branches — low-rank and kernel — reach linearity by approximating the whole matrix rather than sparsifying it.
