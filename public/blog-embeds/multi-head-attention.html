Phase 1 · Attention  /  many heads

One head averages. Many heads specialize.

A single attention head can form exactly one weighted average per token — one relationship. But language needs several at once: agreement, adjacency, the matching earlier mention. Multi-head attention runs many small attentions in parallel, each in its own subspace, for the price of one. Here is the full mechanism: the head split, the output projection W_O, what individual heads end up doing, and every tensor shape along the way.

The scaled dot-product attention we built has a quiet limitation. For a given token, it produces a single softmax distribution over the sequence and reads out a single weighted average of values. That is one relationship per position. But consider "the keys to the cabinet, which she lost, were found." To represent were well, a model wants to attend to keys (subject–verb agreement, far to the left), to lost (the intervening clause), and to the immediately preceding token (local fluency) — three different relationships, at the same position, at once.

A single head cannot do this. A softmax gives one distribution; blending "attend to keys" and "attend to the previous token" into one distribution produces a muddy average of both and captures neither cleanly. And making the head wider (a bigger dkd_kdk​) does not help — it is still one distribution, one average, just computed in a higher-dimensional space.

The one idea

Multi-head attention runs hhh independent attention operations in parallel, each in its own learned subspace of size dk=dmodel/hd_k = d_{\text{model}}/hdk​=dmodel​/h. Because the dimension is split, not multiplied, the whole thing costs about the same as one full-width head — yet the model can attend to hhh different things simultaneously. A final projection WOW_OWO​ mixes the heads' outputs back together. As the paper puts it, "with a single attention head, averaging inhibits this."

We will split one head into many, track every tensor shape as it flows through, see what the heads actually specialize into, and reassemble them with WOW_OWO​.

Each head then runs the entire scaled dot-product attention independently in its own subspace: its own scores, its own softmax, its own value average. Because query and key vectors are learned per head, each head is free to define its own notion of "relevant" — one can learn to point at the previous token, another at the agreeing subject, another at a matching earlier phrase.

Why this is (almost) free Eight heads sounds like eight times the work. It isn't: each head operates in 1/h1/h1/h of the dimension, so the total FLOPs across all heads land close to a single full-width head. The paper notes "the total computational cost is similar to that of single-head attention with full dimensionality." You buy hhh parallel relationships and pay roughly once.

Now each head produces its own n×dvn \times d_vn×dv​ output. We need to fuse the eight verdicts back into one dmodeld_{\text{model}}dmodel​-wide representation.

Concatenate, then mix with W_O

Each head returns an n×dvn \times d_vn×dv​ matrix. Stick them side by side — concatenate along the feature dimension — to get back an n×(h dv)=n×dmodeln \times (h\,d_v) = n \times d_{\text{model}}n×(hdv​)=n×dmodel​ matrix (since h⋅dv=h⋅dmodel/h=dmodelh \cdot d_v = h \cdot d_{\text{model}}/h = d_{\text{model}}h⋅dv​=h⋅dmodel​/h=dmodel​). Then apply one more learned linear map, the output projection WOW_OWO​:

MultiHead(X)=Concat(head1,…,headh) WO,WO∈Rhdv×dmodel.\mathrm{MultiHead}(X) = \mathrm{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h)\, W_O, \qquad W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{h d_v \times d_{\text{model}}}.MultiHead(X)=Concat(head1​,…,headh​)WO​,WO​∈Rhdv​×dmodel​.

WOW_OWO​ is not bookkeeping — it does real work. Concatenation just parks the heads' outputs in disjoint slots; WOW_OWO​ then lets every output dimension be a learned mixture of all heads' findings, and writes the result back into the residual stream at full width. It is what lets head 3's "previous token" signal and head 6's "matching earlier mention" signal be combined into a single useful update, rather than left sitting in separate lanes. A common way to read it: the heads gather information in parallel, and WOW_OWO​ decides how to spend it.

What different heads learn

Trained transformers do not use their heads interchangeably. Probing studies (Voita et al., 2019; Clark et al., 2019) and mechanistic interpretability (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022) find heads settling into recognizable, often reusable roles. A few that show up again and again:

  • Previous-token heads — attend from each position to the one just before it. A simple, ubiquitous building block for local context.
  • Positional / adjacency heads — attend by fixed offset or to nearby tokens regardless of content; also heads that park on delimiters like commas and separators.
  • Induction heads — the star of in-context learning. Given a repeated pattern [A][B]…[A][A][B]\dots[A][A][B]…[A], an induction head attends from the second [A][A][A] to the token that followed the first one and predicts [B][B][B] again. Olsson et al. (2022) argue these are the primary mechanism behind transformers' ability to learn from their own context, and show they emerge in a sharp phase change during training.
  • Syntactic and rare-word heads — Voita et al. find heads that track specific dependency relations (subject→verb, adjective→noun) or reliably point at the least-frequent token in the sentence.
An induction head is a two-head circuit Induction is not one head's doing — it is a circuit. A previous-token head in an earlier layer copies each token's identity onto the next position (prefix matching); a induction head in a later layer reads that and attends to "the token after the previous copy of me," then copies it forward. It is the cleanest known example of heads in different layers composing into an algorithm — and the flagship result of the transformer-circuits program.

What it costs and where it bends

  • Many heads are redundant. Voita et al. (2019) pruned 38 of 48 encoder heads from a trained translation model for a drop of only 0.15 BLEU — the specialized heads (positional, syntactic, rare-word) do the heavy lifting and are pruned last, while the rest are near-dispensable. More heads is not linearly more capability.
  • Head interpretability is partial and post-hoc. The clean stories above are the legible minority; plenty of heads have diffuse patterns that resist a one-line description, and a high attention weight is where a head read, not a proof of why the model decided something.
  • The KV cache scales with heads. At inference each head stores its own keys and values, so the memory bottleneck grows with hhh. This is exactly what multi-query and grouped-query attention attack — sharing one (or a few) key/value heads across many query heads to shrink the cache.
  • Splitting can be too aggressive. Push hhh very high and dkd_kdk​ becomes tiny, leaving each head too little room to represent a useful query/key space. There is a sweet spot, not a free lunch, in "more heads."

Where this goes next

Multi-head attention is the last piece of the attention sublayer. Wrap it in what surrounds it and you have the full transformer block: a residual connection and normalization around the attention (so it edits the residual stream rather than replacing it — the same stream those induction-head circuits read and write), then a position-wise feed-forward network, the whole thing stacked NNN times. That block, repeated, is the transformer.

Two threads run onward. Efficiency: the per-head KV cache motivates grouped-query attention and the rest of the inference-optimization story. Interpretability: the head roles here — previous-token, induction — are the vocabulary of mechanistic interpretability, the project of reading a trained model's learned algorithms off its weights. Everything upstream now composes: tokens → embeddings → multi-head, causally masked attention → the next-token head.
