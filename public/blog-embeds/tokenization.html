Bedrock  /  what the model actually reads

A language model has never seen a letter

It reads integers. Before training, a tokenizer chops all text into chunks from a fixed vocabulary and hands the model their ID numbers. That single lossy step — which letter-clusters get fused into one atom — quietly decides what the model can and cannot reason about. Here is how tokenizers are built, from character-level to byte-level BPE, and why they are behind a startling number of LLM failures.

Ask a frontier model how many times the letter r appears in strawberry and it will, embarrassingly often, say two. This gets passed around as evidence that LLMs "can't reason". It is nothing of the sort. It is a perception bug, and it happens one layer below reasoning — in tokenization.

Here is what the model is actually handed when you type strawberry:

strawberry  → IDs like [str, aw, berry] → [1618, 707, 15717]

Three chunks, each a single opaque integer. The individual letters — the three r's — are fused inside those chunks and are not separately visible to the network any more than you can taste the individual flour molecules in a baked cake. Asking the model to count letters is asking it to read something it was never given. split & IDs illustrative

The one idea

A token is the atom of perception for a language model: the smallest thing it can see. Everything below the token — individual letters, individual digits, byte boundaries — is invisible unless it happens to be its own token. Tokenization is the lens that decides where those atoms fall, and it is built before the model ever trains.

So the real questions are: what should an atom be? how do you choose the vocabulary of atoms from raw text? and what breaks because of the choice? We will answer all three, and you will train a real (tiny) tokenizer by hand along the way.

Two obvious atoms, and why both fail

The tokenizer's job: map text to a sequence of IDs from a fixed vocabulary of size VVV, and back. Two granularities suggest themselves immediately.

Atom = character

Let every character be a token. The vocabulary is tiny (~100 symbols for English), and — crucially — nothing is ever out-of-vocabulary; any string is spellable. But the sequences are brutally long. "internationalization" is one concept and twenty tokens. Since a transformer's attention cost grows with the square of the sequence length, long sequences are expensive, and worse, each token carries almost no meaning on its own — the model must spend its early layers just reassembling words before it can think about them. Character models work but waste enormous capacity on clerical reassembly.

Atom = word

Let every word be a token. Sequences are short and each token is meaningful. But now the vocabulary explodes — English has hundreds of thousands of word forms — and it is still never enough. Every new name, typo, hashtag, or compound (tokenization, SolidGoldMagikarp, covfefe) is an out-of-vocabulary token the model can only render as a useless <UNK>. Morphology is thrown away too: run, runs, running, and ran become four unrelated atoms sharing nothing, so the model must relearn their kinship from scratch. Word-level tokenization is short but lossy and brittle.

The tension in one line Characters give a small, complete vocabulary but long, meaningless sequences. Words give short, meaningful sequences but an enormous, incomplete vocabulary. We want the good half of each: short-ish sequences, a bounded vocabulary, and no out-of-vocabulary failures.

The fix is to let the atom be a subword — and to learn, from data, exactly which letter-clusters are common enough to deserve their own atom.

Byte-Pair Encoding: let the data pick the atoms

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), borrowed from a 1994 data-compression trick and adapted to tokenization by Sennrich, Haddow, and Birch in 2016, resolves the tension with a greedy rule that is almost suspiciously simple:

The BPE training rule

Start with every word split into individual characters. Then repeat: find the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols in the whole corpus, and merge it into one new symbol. Stop when the vocabulary reaches the size you wanted.

That is the entire algorithm. Common letter-clusters (th, ing, tion) get merged early and become single atoms; rare words never accumulate enough pair-frequency and stay in pieces. Frequent words end up as one token, rare ones as a handful of subwords, and — because we started from characters — nothing is ever out-of-vocabulary. You get the short sequences of word-level and the completeness of character-level, with a dial (the target vocabulary size) to trade between them.

Watch it happen: a worked example

Take a toy corpus of five distinct words with these counts: hug×10, pug×5, pun×12, bun×4, hugs×5. Split each into characters, then count every adjacent pair across the corpus, weighting by word frequency. The pair ("u","g") appears in hug, pug, and hugs — a total of 10+5+5=2010 + 5 + 5 = 2010+5+5=20 times, the most of any pair. So the first merge is u g → ug. Recount, and ("u","n") now leads at 12+4=1612 + 4 = 1612+4=16; merge it. Recount again, and ("h","ug") leads at 10+5=1510 + 5 = 1510+5=15; merge that. The vocabulary has grown by three learned atoms — ug, un, hug — each earned by frequency.

Rather than take my word for the counts, run the algorithm yourself. Each press of Merge finds the current most-frequent pair, fuses it everywhere, and updates the vocabulary. Then type a word into the tokenizer box and watch the learned merges apply to it, in order.

Two loose ends remain: what happens to characters the tokenizer never saw during training, and how do we keep merges from gluing across word boundaries. GPT-2 answered both.

Byte-level BPE: the trick every modern tokenizer inherited

In 2019, GPT-2 made one change that fixed out-of-vocabulary tokens forever: start from raw bytes, not characters. Any text in any language — English, Chinese, emoji, source code, a corrupted binary blob — is ultimately a sequence of bytes, and there are exactly 256 possible byte values. Seed the base vocabulary with all 256 of them and every conceivable string is representable, because in the worst case a token falls back to its individual bytes. There is no <UNK>, ever. BPE then merges byte-pairs upward exactly as before.

This is why GPT-2's vocabulary size is the oddly specific 50,257, a number worth decomposing because it is the recipe:

256⏟raw byte values  +  50,000⏟learned BPE merges  +  1⏟<|endoftext|>  =  50,257.\underbrace{256}_{\text{raw byte values}} \;+\; \underbrace{50{,}000}_{\text{learned BPE merges}} \;+\; \underbrace{1}_{\texttt{<|endoftext|>}} \;=\; 50{,}257.raw byte values256​​+learned BPE merges50,000​​+<|endoftext|>1​​=50,257.

256 base atoms guarantee completeness; 50,000 merges buy back short sequences for common English; one special token marks document boundaries. Every major tokenizer since — GPT-3, GPT-4's cl100k, Llama, and most others — is a byte-level BPE that differs mainly in the merge count and the pre-tokenization rules.

Pre-tokenization: don't merge across the spaces

One subtlety makes byte-level BPE behave. Before counting pairs, GPT-2 first splits the text with a regular expression into rough chunks — runs of letters, runs of digits, runs of whitespace, punctuation — and only ever merges within a chunk. Without this, BPE would happily learn a single token for "the " including its trailing space, or fuse the end of one word with the start of the next, producing a vocabulary riddled with alignment quirks. Pre-tokenization is also why a leading space is usually part of the following token: to the tokenizer, " token" (with space) and "token" (without) are different atoms — a fact that quietly bites anyone who splits prompts on word boundaries.

BPE builds a vocabulary bottom-up by gluing. A rival family does the opposite — start huge and carve away — and it powers most non-GPT models.

The other lineage: WordPiece and Unigram

BPE is one of three subword schemes you will meet. They agree on the goal (a bounded subword vocabulary with no OOV) and differ on the rule for building it.

SchemeUsed byHow the vocabulary is builtDirection
BPEGPT-2/3/4, Llamamerge the most frequent adjacent pair, repeatedlybuild up
WordPieceBERT, DistilBERTmerge the pair that most increases corpus likelihood (frequency of pair ÷ frequency of parts)build up
UnigramT5, ALBERT, most SentencePiece modelsstart with a huge vocabulary, iteratively remove the tokens whose loss hurts corpus likelihood leastprune down

WordPiece (BERT) is BPE with a smarter merge score: instead of raw pair frequency it merges the pair that most improves the likelihood of the corpus under a unigram model, which is the frequency of the pair divided by the product of its parts' frequencies. This favors pairs that co-occur more than chance would predict, not merely pairs that are common because their parts are common.

Unigram (Kudo, 2018), usually delivered via Google's SentencePiece library, runs the logic backwards. It seeds an intentionally oversized vocabulary of candidate subwords, fits a probabilistic model in which every token has a probability and a word's segmentation is chosen to maximize likelihood, then repeatedly drops the tokens whose removal costs the least likelihood — pruning down to the target size. Because it is probabilistic, it can represent a word by multiple candidate segmentations with different probabilities (useful for regularization), and because SentencePiece treats the input as a raw stream and encodes spaces as a visible ▁ marker, it needs no language-specific pre-tokenization — a big reason it dominates multilingual and non-whitespace languages.

Rule of thumb BPE and WordPiece grow a vocabulary by gluing common pieces together; Unigram carves one out of an oversized set by deleting the least useful pieces. At a 32K vocabulary the two families perform within a whisker of each other; their differences show up at the extremes of vocabulary size and in multilingual settings.

The vocabulary-size dial

Every tokenizer decision collapses into one number you must choose up front: VVV, the vocabulary size. It is a genuine trade-off with costs on both sides, which is why there is no single right answer and why it has crept from ~30K in the BERT era toward 128K–256K in recent models.

Bigger VVV shortens sequences. More and longer atoms means the same document becomes fewer tokens. Since attention cost scales with the square of sequence length, and since a fixed context window then holds more text, this is a real and compounding win — fewer steps to generate, more document per context.

Bigger VVV inflates the model and starves rare tokens. The token-embedding table and the output softmax each have one row per vocabulary entry, so their parameter count is

embedding + unembedding params  =  2 V dmodel,\text{embedding + unembedding params} \;=\; 2 \, V \, d_{\text{model}},embedding + unembedding params=2Vdmodel​,

linear in VVV. At dmodel=4096d_{\text{model}} = 4096dmodel​=4096, going from a 32K to a 256K vocabulary grows just these two tables from ~0.26B to ~2.1B parameters — before the transformer proper. And a rare atom that appears a handful of times in training gets a poorly estimated embedding: push VVV too high and you mint thousands of under-trained tokens (which, as we are about to see, is how monsters are made).

Why tokenization causes weird LLM failures

Everything above pays off here. A striking share of "the model is dumb" moments are really "the model was handed the wrong atoms" moments. Five of the most common:

1. Spelling and letter-counting

The strawberry problem from the top. The model reasons over tokens, and letters are fused inside them, so any task that requires per-letter access — counting letters, reversing a string, finding rhymes, pig latin — fights the representation. The model can sometimes reconstruct spelling from memorized associations, but it is working against the grain of what it can perceive, not exercising or failing at logic.

2. Arithmetic

Numbers are tokenized by the same frequency-driven BPE as words, which chops them into arbitrary multi-digit chunks: 1234 might be one token while 1235 is two, and the grouping carries no place-value structure. A model that sees 327 and 328 as unrelated atoms with no shared "hundreds digit" has to memorize arithmetic facts rather than compute them. It is no accident that several recent tokenizers deliberately force numbers to split into single digits, restoring a consistent per-digit representation and measurably improving arithmetic.

3. Glitch tokens — the SolidGoldMagikarp affair

In 2023, researchers found tokens that made GPT-2 and GPT-3 behave bizarrely — refusing to repeat them, emitting unrelated words, insulting the user, breaking determinism. The most infamous was SolidGoldMagikarp; others included TheNitromeFan, RandomRedditorWithNo, and BuyableInstoreAndOnline. The cause is pure tokenization pathology: the BPE vocabulary was trained on a corpus (heavy with Reddit and scraped web junk) in which these strings were common enough to earn their own token — but the language-model training data barely contained them. So the model held atoms whose embeddings were almost never trained, leaving them as un-optimized noise vectors pointing in random directions of representation space. Feed one in and the model has, quite literally, no idea what it is looking at.

The lesson under the monster Glitch tokens are what happens when the tokenizer and the model are trained on different data. The vocabulary is frozen before training, so any mismatch mints atoms the network never learns to interpret. It is a vivid reminder that the tokenizer is a real, separately-trained component with its own failure modes — not a neutral pass-through.

4. Multilingual inequality

A tokenizer trained mostly on English spends its 50,000 merges buying short tokens for English words. The same sentence in a language it saw less of — Hindi, Burmese, Thai — falls back toward bytes and can cost several times as many tokens. Because you pay per token and the context window is counted in tokens, speakers of under-represented languages get higher bills, slower responses, and less usable context for the identical meaning. Tokenization bakes a fairness gap in before a single weight is trained.

5. Prompt-boundary brittleness

Because " word" and "word" are different atoms, a trailing space in a prompt, or splitting a few-shot example on the wrong boundary, can shift the model onto token sequences it rarely saw in training and degrade output for no reason visible in the text. "Never end your prompt with a space" is folklore with a precise tokenization cause.

Where this goes next

Two threads lead out. The first is the recurring question of whether we need tokenization at all: byte-level and token-free models (from ByT5 to recent byte-latent approaches) push the atom all the way down to raw bytes and let the network learn the chunking, trading tokenizer pathologies for longer sequences and more compute. The tokenizer is one of the last hand-designed, non-learned components in the stack, and there is steady pressure to dissolve it.

The second connects straight back to the companion post on language modeling and perplexity. Recall that perplexity is a per-token quantity — which is exactly why you cannot compare the perplexities of two models with different tokenizers. A model with a larger vocabulary spreads the same text over fewer, individually-harder tokens; its per-token surprise is not measuring the same thing. The atom you choose here silently sets the units of everything the model is trained and judged on. Tokenization is not preprocessing. It is the ground floor.
