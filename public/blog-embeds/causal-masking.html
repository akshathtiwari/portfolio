The one line that lets a model write left to right

Self-attention lets every token see every other token — including the ones it is supposed to predict. A decoder-only transformer fixes this with a single trick: set the future to −∞ before the softmax. That one mask is what makes autoregressive generation possible, what lets training run in a single parallel pass, and where teacher forcing and its famous failure mode both come from.

In the last post we built scaled dot-product attention and noted, in one sentence, that a decoder masks the future. That sentence hides the load-bearing idea of the whole decoder-only architecture. Here is why it is not optional.

You want to train a language model on the sentence The cat sat on the mat. The efficient thing is to predict every next token in a single forward pass: at position The predict cat, at cat predict sat, and so on — six predictions, one pass, one big gradient. But self-attention is promiscuous: it lets position The attend to every token in the sequence, including cat — the very token it is supposed to guess. The answer is sitting in the input.

The one idea

A causal mask forbids every position from attending to any position to its right, by setting those attention scores to −∞ before the softmax. This makes a single parallel forward pass compute, at every position, exactly the prediction it would make if it had only seen the past. Training (all positions at once, on true tokens) and inference (one token at a time, on its own tokens) become the same function — and that identity is the entire decoder-only design.

We will watch the leak happen, plug it with −∞, and then trace how the very same mask produces both the efficiency of parallel training and the subtle bug that haunts generation.

The leak: attention can read the answer

Recall the attention scores S=QK⊤S = QK^\topS=QK⊤, where SijS_{ij}Sij​ is how much the token at position iii attends to the token at position jjj. In an unmasked layer, jjj ranges over the whole sequence — past, present, and future. So when position iii builds its output representation, that representation can depend on token i+1i+1i+1.

Now recall the training target: the output at position iii is used to predict token i+1i+1i+1. Put those together and the catastrophe is obvious — the model is asked to predict token i+1i+1i+1 from a representation that is allowed to look at token i+1i+1i+1. It will learn the trivial shortcut: copy the next token through the attention path. Training loss plummets to near zero, and the model is worthless, because at generation time token i+1i+1i+1 does not exist yet — it is the thing we are trying to produce. This is label leakage, and an autoregressive model trained with full attention learns nothing but to cheat.

The fix must make the future unreachable — not softly discouraged, but exactly, arithmetically absent from every position's attention.

The fix: set the future to −∞ before softmax

Why before the softmax, not after You might try zeroing the future weights after the softmax instead. That fails: the softmax would have already spent probability mass on the future, and zeroing afterward leaves the surviving weights summing to less than 1 — an un-normalized, leaky distribution. Masking with −∞-\infty−∞ before the softmax makes the normalization itself ignore the future, so the kept weights are a proper distribution over the legal positions.

The payoff: one pass trains every position

With the future masked, something wonderful becomes possible. Because position iii now depends only on tokens ≤i\le i≤i, a single forward pass over the whole sequence computes, simultaneously and independently, the correct next-token prediction for every position — each one honestly conditioned on just its past. You do not loop over positions; you run the sequence once and read off nnn predictions.

Training a decoder-only LM is then just: take a sequence, shift it by one to make inputs and targets, run one masked pass, and average the cross-entropy over all positions.

inputs=(x1,…,xT−1),targets=(x2,…,xT),L=−1T−1∑t=1T−1log⁡pθ(xt+1∣x≤t).\text{inputs} = (x_1, \dots, x_{T-1}), \quad \text{targets} = (x_2, \dots, x_T), \quad \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \log p_\theta(x_{t+1} \mid x_{\le t}).inputs=(x1​,…,xT−1​),targets=(x2​,…,xT​),L=−T−11​t=1∑T−1​logpθ​(xt+1​∣x≤t​).

Feeding the model the true previous tokens as context at every step — rather than its own predictions — is called teacher forcing (Williams & Zipser, 1989). It is what lets all positions be computed at once: since every position's input is the ground-truth prefix, none of them has to wait for another's output. Teacher forcing plus the causal mask is why a transformer trains on a whole sequence in one parallel, GPU-friendly shot — the efficiency the recurrent models before it could never reach.

Training vs inference: the same mask, two worlds

At training time the entire target sequence is known, so teacher forcing feeds the real tokens and the mask enforces the rule "don't peek right." At inference time the future genuinely does not exist yet: the model must generate token by token, appending each output to the input and running again. The very same causal mask now enforces a fact — there is nothing to the right to attend to. The constraint the mask simulated during training becomes reality during generation. The seam: exposure bias

That switch between worlds is not free. During training the model only ever sees correct prefixes (teacher forcing). During generation it sees its own prefixes, which may contain mistakes. The moment it emits a token it never would have seen after a correct history, it is conditioning on a context unlike anything in training — and errors can compound down the sequence. Bengio et al. (2015) named this train–test mismatch exposure bias: the model is exposed to different data distributions during training and inference.

The honest cost of teacher forcing Teacher forcing buys the parallel-training efficiency that made transformers practical, but it trains the model only on the distribution of true prefixes, never on the distribution of its own generations. Fixes exist — scheduled sampling (mixing in the model's own tokens during training), and at the sequence level, RL-style objectives — but plain autoregressive LMs live with some exposure bias, which is one reason a low teacher-forced perplexity does not guarantee good free-running generation.

The mask is also the exact dividing line between the two great transformer families — and it is a one-character change.

Encoder vs decoder: the mask is the whole difference

Remove the mask and every position sees the entire sequence, in both directions. That is a bidirectional encoder (BERT and friends): superb at understanding a fixed input — classification, tagging, retrieval — because each token is informed by full left and right context. But it cannot generate autoregressively, because it has no notion of "the past so far."

Add the mask and you have a causal decoder (GPT and essentially every generative LLM): each token sees only its past, so the model defines a proper left-to-right probability over sequences and can generate one token at a time. Same attention math, same weights shapes — the presence or absence of that one lower-triangular mask is what separates a model that reads from a model that writes.

What it costs and what it doesn't

  • It does not save the O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2). The mask zeroes the upper triangle of the score matrix but you still, in a naive implementation, compute all n2n^2n2 scores. (Good kernels like FlashAttention skip fully-masked blocks, recovering roughly the factor of two, but the asymptotics stand.)
  • It enables the KV cache. Because token iii only ever attends to tokens ≤i\le i≤i, the keys and values of earlier tokens never change as generation proceeds — so you compute them once and cache them, making each generation step attend to stored keys/values instead of recomputing them. Causality is precisely what makes that caching valid.
  • It forfeits right context for generation. A decoder can never use future tokens, by design; tasks that benefit from bidirectional context (some infilling, editing) need encoder-style attention or special masking schemes.
  • Teacher forcing ≠ free-running. As above, the training regime and the generation regime differ, and the gap (exposure bias) is real if usually tolerable.

Where this goes next

The causal mask is the hinge between the two things a transformer does. Two threads lead straight out. The first is the KV cache and the broader machinery of efficient autoregressive inference — the optimization the mask makes possible, and the reason serving a token costs what it costs. The second is decoding itself: this post produced one probability distribution per step, but turning a stream of distributions into text means sampling from them (greedy, temperature, top-kkk, nucleus), where the exposure-bias seam becomes something you actively manage.

Everything upstream now connects: tokens become embeddings, attention with a causal mask mixes them left-to-right, and the next-token objective scores the result — trained in one masked, teacher-forced pass and unrolled, one token at a time, to write.
