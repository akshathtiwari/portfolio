The attention matrix is mostly redundant

Sparse methods pick which pairs to compute. The Linformer takes a different route: it observes that the whole n×n attention matrix is approximately low-rank — its information lives in a tiny subspace — so you can compress the keys and values from n tokens down to k ≪ n and attend to those. n×n becomes n×k, and the cost falls to O(n).

The Reformer saved compute by only scoring each query against its likely near-neighbours — it kept attention exact on a chosen subset. The Linformer (Wang et al., 2020) makes a bolder bet: that you don't need the full attention matrix at all, because most of it is redundant.

Their evidence is spectral. Take a trained transformer's n×nn \times nn×n attention matrix P=softmax(QK⊤/d)P = \mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d})P=softmax(QK⊤/d​) and look at its singular values: they decay fast. Almost all of the matrix's "energy" sits in the top handful of singular directions, and the rest is nearly noise. In other words PPP is approximately low-rank — well-approximated by a matrix of rank kkk for some small constant kkk, independent of the sequence length.

The one idea

If the n×nn \times nn×n attention matrix is approximately rank-kkk with k≪nk \ll nk≪n, you never need nnn keys. Insert two learned matrices that project the key and value sequences from length nnn down to length kkk along the sequence dimension, and attend to those kkk "summary" tokens. The attention shape becomes n×kn \times kn×k instead of n×nn \times nn×n, so time and memory are O(nk)=O(n)O(nk) = O(n)O(nk)=O(n).

Low-rank is not a wish — it's a measured property, with a theoretical backstop.

Low-rank, and why a small k suffices

The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma says you can project high-dimensional points to a much smaller dimension while approximately preserving all their pairwise distances — the target dimension depends on the desired error, not on the number of points. Linformer applies this to the attention computation: the softmax attention matrix can be approximated to error ε\varepsilonε by a rank-kkk matrix with k=O(d/ε2)k = O(d/\varepsilon^2)k=O(d/ε2) — crucially independent of nnn. So as sequences get longer, kkk stays fixed.

The mechanism: two projection matrices

Standard attention uses keys KKK and values VVV, each n×dn \times dn×d. Linformer adds two learned projection matrices E,F∈Rk×nE, F \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times n}E,F∈Rk×n that shrink the sequence dimension from nnn to kkk:

K′=E K∈Rk×d,V′=F V∈Rk×d.K' = E\,K \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}, \qquad V' = F\,V \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}.K′=EK∈Rk×d,V′=FV∈Rk×d.

Then attention runs against these compressed keys and values:

Attention=softmax ⁣(Q K′⊤d)⏟n×k  V′.\mathrm{Attention} = \underbrace{\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q\,K'^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right)}_{n \times k}\; V'.Attention=n×ksoftmax(d​QK′⊤​)​​V′.

The queries stay full-length (n×dn \times dn×d), but they now attend to only kkk compressed keys, so the attention matrix is n×kn \times kn×k — a tall, thin strip instead of a giant square. EEE and FFF are trained end-to-end; they learn which kkk combinations of positions to summarize into. The projections can even be shared across heads and layers to save parameters.

Why it is O(n)

The expensive product QK′⊤Q K'^\topQK′⊤ is (n×d)(d×k)=O(nkd)(n \times d)(d \times k) = O(nkd)(n×d)(d×k)=O(nkd); the softmax over an n×kn \times kn×k matrix is O(nk)O(nk)O(nk); the final multiply by V′V'V′ is again O(nkd)O(nkd)O(nkd). With kkk a fixed constant, every term is linear in nnn, and memory is O(nk)O(nk)O(nk) — no n×nn \times nn×n matrix is ever formed. What it costs

  • The sequence length is baked in. Because E,FE, FE,F are k×nk \times nk×n, the projection is tied to a specific nnn. Linformer wants a fixed or bounded sequence length — perfect for encoders (classification, retrieval on padded inputs), awkward for autoregressive generation, where nnn grows one token at a time and a global length-nnn projection is ill-defined.
  • Global compression loses precise reach. The low-rank bet fails on tasks that need genuinely high-rank attention — exact copying, precise long-range retrieval — where a specific distant token must be attended to sharply rather than through a blurry summary.
  • It approximates, and the error is real when the attention matrix is not actually low-rank (early layers, some heads).

Where this sits in the lineage

Linformer defines the low-rank / projection branch of efficient attention: don't skip pairs (sparse), and don't hash them (Reformer) — compress the whole interaction into a smaller matrix. Its sibling is the kernel branch, which reaches O(n)O(n)O(n) a different way — by rewriting the softmax itself as a product of feature maps so the n×nn \times nn×n matrix is never needed. That is the Performer, the next post. Both accept an approximation for linear cost; both trade some of full attention's exactness for scale.
