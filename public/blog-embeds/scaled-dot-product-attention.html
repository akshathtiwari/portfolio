Attention is a soft dictionary lookup

Every token asks a question, every token advertises what it offers, and each one reads back a blend of answers weighted by how well the questions match. That is scaled dot-product attention in full: what the Q, K, V projections are, why a dot product measures relevance, why we divide by √dₖ (derived, not asserted), and a 3-token example computed to the last decimal.

read  ~21 min math  variance derivation interactive  √dₖ · steer a query series  Attention · 1

The previous post ended on a cliffhanger: attention gives any position a direct, one-step path to any other, and once you have that, the recurrent machinery becomes dead weight. That is why the transformer exists. This post opens the box and shows exactly how the one-step path is built.

Consider the word bank in "she sat on the river bank." To represent it correctly, the model must pull in river and ignore sat — and in "she opened a bank account," it must pull in account instead. The information it needs lives at a specific other position, chosen by content, not by fixed distance. Attention is the mechanism that lets each token reach out and gather from exactly the positions it finds relevant.

The one idea

Attention is a differentiable, content-addressed dictionary lookup. Each position emits a query ("what am I looking for?"), every position exposes a key ("what do I match?") and a value ("what will I hand over?"). The query is compared against every key by dot product to get relevance weights, and the output is the weighted average of the values. The only non-obvious detail — dividing by √dₖ — is what keeps the lookup soft enough to train.

We will build that sentence into equations one clause at a time, and you will get to break the √dₖ scaling on a slider and steer a query around a map of keys to watch it select.

Q, K, V: three views of the same tokens

The input is a sequence of nnn token vectors (the embeddings, plus position), stacked as rows of a matrix X∈Rn×dX \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}X∈Rn×d. A single token vector plays three different roles in a lookup — asker, advertiser, provider — so we give it three different projections, each a learned weight matrix:

Q=X WQ,K=X WK,V=X WV,\textcolor{#a78bfa}{Q} = X\,W_Q, \qquad \textcolor{#f2c14e}{K} = X\,W_K, \qquad \textcolor{#5ed3a6}{V} = X\,W_V,Q=XWQ​,K=XWK​,V=XWV​,

with WQ,WK∈Rd×dkW_Q, W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_k}WQ​,WK​∈Rd×dk​ and WV∈Rd×dvW_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_v}WV​∈Rd×dv​. So Q, K, and V are each an n×dkn \times d_kn×dk​ (or n×dvn \times d_vn×dv​) matrix with one row per token. Row iii of Q is token iii's query vector qiq_iqi​; row jjj of K is token jjj's key kjk_jkj​; row jjj of V is its value vjv_jvj​.

Why three separate matrices instead of using the raw embedding three times? Because the three roles are genuinely different. The features that make bank a good question about nearby nouns need not be the features that make river a good answer to that question. Splitting into learned query, key, and value spaces lets the model learn "what to look for," "what to match on," and "what to pass along" independently. In the original transformer, d=512d = 512d=512 is split across 8 heads so each uses dk=dv=64d_k = d_v = 64dk​=dv​=64.

With every token carrying a query and a key, the next question is how to score one against another. That score has to mean "relevance."

Why the dot product measures similarity

To score how well query qiq_iqi​ matches key kjk_jkj​, attention uses their dot product. The reason it captures relevance is geometric — the dot product factors into magnitudes and the cosine of the angle between the two vectors:

qi⋅kj  =  ∑m=1dkqi,m kj,m  =  ∥qi∥ ∥kj∥cos⁡θ.q_i \cdot k_j \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{d_k} q_{i,m}\,k_{j,m} \;=\; \lVert q_i \rVert\,\lVert k_j \rVert \cos\theta.qi​⋅kj​=m=1∑dk​​qi,m​kj,m​=∥qi​∥∥kj​∥cosθ.

When the query and key point the same way (cos⁡θ→1\cos\theta \to 1cosθ→1) the score is large and positive; when they are orthogonal (cos⁡θ=0\cos\theta = 0cosθ=0) it is zero; when they oppose, it is negative. So the dot product is an unnormalized cosine similarity, and because WQW_QWQ​ and WKW_KWK​ are learned, training gets to define what "pointing the same way" means: it shapes the query and key spaces so that a token's query aligns with the keys of exactly the tokens it should attend to.

Compute this for every query–key pair at once and you get the score matrix, a single matrix multiply:

S  =  Q K⊤  ∈  Rn×n,Sij=qi⋅kj.S \;=\; \textcolor{#a78bfa}{Q}\,\textcolor{#f2c14e}{K}^{\top} \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}, \qquad S_{ij} = q_i \cdot k_j.S=QK⊤∈Rn×n,Sij​=qi​⋅kj​.

Entry SijS_{ij}Sij​ is how much token iii (the querying row) finds token jjj (a key column) relevant. This n×nn \times nn×n matrix — every token scored against every token — is exactly the "connect any two positions in one step" that makes attention special, and also exactly the O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) cost that the rest of the field spends years fighting.

Left alone, these raw scores have a scale problem that quietly breaks training — and the fix is the most famous constant in the transformer.

The √dₖ scaling, derived

Here is the failure the 1/dk1/\sqrt{d_k}1/dk​​ factor prevents. The scores feed a softmax, and softmax is only "soft" while its inputs are modest in magnitude; feed it inputs of large magnitude and it saturates — one entry goes to (almost) 1, the rest to (almost) 0 — at which point its gradient is nearly zero and learning stalls. So we need to know how big the raw dot products typically get. They grow with dimension, and we can show exactly how fast.

Assume, as the paper does, that the components of a query and key are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1. Then the dot product q⋅k=∑m=1dkqmkmq \cdot k = \sum_{m=1}^{d_k} q_m k_mq⋅k=∑m=1dk​​qm​km​ has mean

E[q⋅k]  =  ∑m=1dkE[qm] E[km]  =  ∑m=1dk0⋅0  =  0,\mathbb{E}[q \cdot k] \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{d_k} \mathbb{E}[q_m]\,\mathbb{E}[k_m] \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{d_k} 0 \cdot 0 \;=\; 0,E[q⋅k]=m=1∑dk​​E[qm​]E[km​]=m=1∑dk​​0⋅0=0,

using independence and zero mean. For the variance, the terms are independent, so variances add; and for a single term, since qmq_mqm​ and kmk_mkm​ are independent with mean 0,

Var(qmkm)=E[qm2km2]−(E[qmkm])2=E[qm2] E[km2]−0=Var(qm) Var(km)=1.\mathrm{Var}(q_m k_m) = \mathbb{E}[q_m^2 k_m^2] - \big(\mathbb{E}[q_m k_m]\big)^2 = \mathbb{E}[q_m^2]\,\mathbb{E}[k_m^2] - 0 = \mathrm{Var}(q_m)\,\mathrm{Var}(k_m) = 1.Var(qm​km​)=E[qm2​km2​]−(E[qm​km​])2=E[qm2​]E[km2​]−0=Var(qm​)Var(km​)=1.

Summing over the dkd_kdk​ independent terms gives the key result:

Var(q⋅k)  =  ∑m=1dkVar(qmkm)  =  dk,sostd(q⋅k)=dk.\mathrm{Var}(q \cdot k) \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{d_k} \mathrm{Var}(q_m k_m) \;=\; d_k, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \mathrm{std}(q \cdot k) = \sqrt{d_k}.Var(q⋅k)=m=1∑dk​​Var(qm​km​)=dk​,sostd(q⋅k)=dk​​.

The typical magnitude of a raw score grows like dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​. With dk=64d_k = 64dk​=64 the scores have a standard deviation of 8 — easily large enough to saturate the softmax. Dividing every score by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ rescales the variance back to 1 regardless of dimension:

Var ⁣(q⋅kdk)=Var(q⋅k)dk=dkdk=1.\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{q \cdot k}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(q\cdot k)}{d_k} = \frac{d_k}{d_k} = 1.Var(dk​​q⋅k​)=dk​Var(q⋅k)​=dk​dk​​=1.

That is the whole justification: keep the score variance at 1 so the softmax stays in its soft, high-gradient regime as dkd_kdk​ scales. Break it yourself below — turn off the scaling and watch the attention collapse to a hard one-hot as dkd_kdk​ grows, taking the gradient with it.

Row-wise softmax, then a weighted sum of values

Now turn scores into a probability distribution — separately for each querying token. Apply softmax along each row of the scaled score matrix, so row iii becomes a set of non-negative weights that sum to 1: token iii's attention distribution over all nnn tokens.

Aij  =  softmaxj ⁣(Sijdk)  =  exp⁡(Sij/dk)∑j′=1nexp⁡(Sij′/dk).A_{ij} \;=\; \mathrm{softmax}_j\!\left(\frac{S_{ij}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) \;=\; \frac{\exp(S_{ij}/\sqrt{d_k})}{\sum_{j'=1}^{n}\exp(S_{ij'}/\sqrt{d_k})}.Aij​=softmaxj​(dk​​Sij​​)=∑j′=1n​exp(Sij′​/dk​​)exp(Sij​/dk​​)​.

The row-wise part matters: each token independently spends one unit of "attention budget" across the sequence. Finally, use each row of weights to take a weighted average of the value vectors — the actual read-out from the dictionary:

Attention(Q,K,V)  =  softmax ⁣(QK⊤dk)⏟A,  n×n V.\mathrm{Attention}(\textcolor{#a78bfa}{Q},\textcolor{#f2c14e}{K},\textcolor{#5ed3a6}{V}) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\textcolor{#a78bfa}{Q}\textcolor{#f2c14e}{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)}_{A,\ \ n \times n}\ \textcolor{#5ed3a6}{V}.Attention(Q,K,V)=A,  n×nsoftmax(dk​​QK⊤​)​​ V.

Output row iii is ∑jAij vj\sum_j A_{ij}\, v_j∑j​Aij​vj​ — a blend of every token's value, weighted by how relevant token iii found it. That single line is scaled dot-product attention in full. Everything else in a transformer is this operation wrapped, stacked, and multiplied across heads.

One detail for language models: the causal mask

In a decoder predicting the next token, position iii must not peek at positions after it, or the task would be trivial (the answer is right there). Attention enforces this with a causal mask: before the softmax, set every "future" score to −∞-\infty−∞,

Sij←−∞for j>i,S_{ij} \leftarrow -\infty \quad \text{for } j > i,Sij​←−∞for j>i,

so exp⁡(−∞)=0\exp(-\infty) = 0exp(−∞)=0 and those positions receive exactly zero weight. Each token attends only to itself and the past. It is a one-line change to the score matrix, and it is the entire difference between an encoder (bidirectional) and a decoder (autoregressive) attention layer.

What it costs and what it can't do alone

  • It is O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2). The score matrix has n2n^2n2 entries; doubling the sequence quadruples the attention compute and memory. This is the price of the one-step path from the last post, and the target of KV caching, FlashAttention, and sliding-window / linear attention.
  • One head averages away detail. A single softmax-weighted average can only express one "kind" of relationship at a time. Resolving bank might need syntactic and semantic attention at once — which is precisely why real models use multi-head attention: several independent Q/K/V projections in parallel, concatenated.
  • Attention weights are not explanations. A high AijA_{ij}Aij​ is where the layer read from, not a faithful account of why the model decided what it did; treat attention maps as suggestive, not as ground-truth interpretability.
  • The variance argument is an idealization. The mean-0, variance-1 assumption holds at initialization; after training, query/key statistics drift, but dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ remains the right order-of-magnitude correction and is universal in practice.

Where this goes next

You now hold the operation every transformer is built from. The immediate sequel is multi-head attention — run hhh of these in parallel with different projections, concatenate, and project once more — which is a few lines on top of what you just derived. Around it goes the rest of the transformer block: a residual connection and layer norm so attention edits the representation rather than replacing it, then a position-wise feed-forward network, stacked NNN times.

Two threads from earlier posts close here. The QK⊤Q K^\topQK⊤ dot product you scaled is the same "alignment by dot product" idea from token embeddings' unembedding head, reused to score positions instead of vocabulary. And the softmax you took row-wise is the same one that turns logits into next-token probabilities. Attention did not invent new primitives — it pointed the ones you already had at a new target: the sequence itself. For a focused deep-dive on just the scaling constant, see the companion post "Why We Scale Attention by √dₖ."
