A Learning Rate
for Your Prompt

You cannot differentiate a block of text. But if you call the skill document a parameter, the task score a loss, and an optimizer model's edits a gradient, you can run a real training loop over language, with a learning rate, a validation gate, and momentum. That is SkillOpt, and it turns a 41.8% agent into an 80.7% one by editing a single file.

The one idea

Give text optimization the discipline of weight optimization. Treat one skill file as the parameter, let an optimizer LLM propose bounded edits as the “gradient,” cap how many you apply per step (the learning rate), and keep an edit only when a held-out score strictly improves. Everything that makes SGD reproducible — a step size, a validation check, momentum, a schedule — ported onto a document.

The metaphor we will keep

You are perfecting one recipe card. Each round you cook for a panel and collect taste scores. A critic tastes the failures and writes specific fixes — cut the salt, add a resting step — but you only let a few edits onto the card per round, and you keep an edit only if a fresh panel scores the new card higher. A side notebook records what already flopped so you never re-try it. The card is the prompt; the critic is the optimizer; how many edits you allow per round is the learning rate.

Gradient descent, in one line

Training a neural network is a loop you already know. You have parameters θ\thetaθ, a loss L(θ)L(\theta)L(θ) that measures how wrong they are, and you nudge the parameters downhill:

θt+1  =  θt  −  η ∇θL(θt)\theta_{t+1} \;=\; \theta_{t} \;-\; \eta\, \nabla_\theta L(\theta_t)θt+1​=θt​−η∇θ​L(θt​)

Three ingredients do all the work. The loss LLL says how bad things are. The gradient ∇θL\nabla_\theta L∇θ​L points in the direction that makes the loss worse, so you step the opposite way. And the learning rate η\etaη controls how far you move — too small and you crawl, too large and you overshoot the valley and bounce.

Now point that machinery at a prompt. The catch is immediate: a prompt is discrete text, not a vector of floats. There is no ∇θL\nabla_\theta L∇θ​L to compute, because you cannot take a derivative of “You are a helpful assistant.” So what plays the role of each ingredient?

The gradient has to come from somewhere. The trick is old, and it is worth meeting first.

The predecessor: a gradient made of words

In 2023, ProTeGi (“Automatic Prompt Optimization with Gradient Descent and Beam Search,” Pryzant et al., EMNLP 2023) made the move that everything since inherits. You cannot differentiate a prompt, but you can ask an LLM to criticize it. Run the prompt on a minibatch, collect the failures, and ask a model: what about this prompt caused these mistakes? The natural-language critique it returns is the textual gradient — it points at what is wrong. Then you “step” by editing the prompt in the opposite semantic direction: rewrite it to fix the criticism. ProTeGi wrapped this in a beam search over candidate prompts and reported gains up to 31%.

That is the conceptual unlock. But there is a gap between “an LLM can criticize a prompt” and “this reliably converges.” Ordinary SGD is reproducible because of a lot of unglamorous machinery: a controlled step size, a held-out check, momentum, a schedule. Strip that away and text optimization becomes a slot machine.

Which is exactly the failure SkillOpt is built around.

The naive loop, and why it drifts

The obvious thing — and what many “self-improving agent” setups do — is to let the agent revise its own instructions in a loop: run, ask it to rewrite its skill, repeat. SkillOpt's framing of why this is not enough is blunt: hand-crafted, one-shot, or loosely self-revised skills are “none of which reliably improves over its starting point under feedback.” Revisions regress as often as they help, because nothing forces each step to be an improvement, and nothing stops the fifth rewrite from undoing the second.

There is also a subtler failure that motivates the whole design, and you can feel it in the recipe: if the critic hands you twenty edits and you apply them all at once, then when the new card scores worse you have no idea which edit did the damage — and when it scores better you have learned nothing transferable. Big steps in a discrete space are unattributable. That is an overshooting learning rate, and it is the thing SkillOpt's edit budget exists to control.

So SkillOpt keeps the textual gradient and wraps it in optimizer discipline. Four pieces.

Piece one: the parameter, the loss, the gradient

The parameter is a single skill document sss — a short natural-language instruction file, the frozen agent's external state. Not a throwaway prompt; a persistent artifact you ship. The loss is one minus the task score:

L(s)  =  1−r(s),r(s)  =  1∣D∣∑x∈Dr(s;x)  ∈  [0,1]L(s) \;=\; 1 - r(s), \qquad r(s) \;=\; \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{x \in \mathcal{D}} r(s; x) \;\in\; [0,1]L(s)=1−r(s),r(s)=∣D∣1​x∈D∑​r(s;x)∈[0,1]

where r(s;x)r(s; x)r(s;x) is the harness score of running the agent with skill sss on task xxx — did the spreadsheet come out right, was the question answered. The gradient is a separate optimizer LLM: it reads full execution traces, splits them into successes and failures, and emits bounded edits on the document — add a line, delete a line, replace a line. Failures produce corrections; successes produce edits that preserve what already works, so the step does not trample a good habit while fixing a bad one.

Now the two pieces that turn a critique into a stable step: how far to move, and whether to keep the move.

Piece two: the learning rate is an edit budget

Here is SkillOpt's cleanest idea. The learning-rate analogue is the edit budget LtL_tLt​ — the maximum number of edits applied at step ttt. The optimizer may propose many; they are merged (failure-fixes first, then success-preserving), ranked by expected utility, and clipped to the top LtL_tLt​. A small budget means each step is a small, attributable move. A large budget means a big, unattributable leap that the validation gate tends to reject wholesale.

And because it is a learning rate, it gets a schedule. SkillOpt supports constant, linear, cosine, and autonomous schedules; the default is a cosine decay from Lt=4L_t = 4Lt​=4 down to a floor of Lt=2L_t = 2Lt​=2 — bold edits early when the skill is empty, careful edits late when you are polishing. Exactly the shape a deep-learning practitioner reaches for. The widget below lets you feel why the middle path wins.

But notice what did the rejecting. That gate is the second half of the discipline.

Piece three: the validation gate

Every candidate skill s~\tilde{s}s~ is scored on a held-out selection split Dsel\mathcal{D}_{sel}Dsel​ — a fresh tasting panel, not the tasks the gradient was computed on. The acceptance rule is strict:

accept s~  ⟺  rsel(s~)  >  rsel(scur)\text{accept } \tilde{s} \iff r_{sel}(\tilde{s}) \;>\; r_{sel}(s_{cur})accept s~⟺rsel​(s~)>rsel​(scur​)

Strictly greater — ties are rejected, so the skill never drifts sideways on noise. If the candidate also beats the best score ever seen, it is saved as best_skill.md. If it fails, it is discarded and its edits drop into a rejected-edit buffer that is fed back to the optimizer as negative evidence: we tried this, it did not help, do not propose it again. That buffer is SkillOpt's momentum — it keeps the search from re-proposing the same dead ends and gives each step a memory of the last.

The formal objective is just “pick the candidate that maximizes held-out score,” with the loop above generating the candidate set C(Dtr)\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{D}_{tr})C(Dtr​):

ssel∗  =  arg⁡max⁡s ∈ C(Dtr)  1∣Dsel∣∑x∈Dselr(s;x)s^{*}_{sel} \;=\; \arg\max_{s \,\in\, \mathcal{C}(\mathcal{D}_{tr})} \; \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{sel}|} \sum_{x \in \mathcal{D}_{sel}} r(s; x)ssel∗​=args∈C(Dtr​)max​∣Dsel​∣1​x∈Dsel​∑​r(s;x)

This monotone-by-construction gate is why SkillOpt reliably improves over its starting point — the property the naive self-revision loop could not promise.

Piece four: the slow update and meta-memory

Everything so far is the fast update path — per-step edits inside an epoch. On top of it SkillOpt runs a slow update path once per epoch (from the second epoch on): it re-samples the same tasks under the skill as it was at the epoch's start versus its end, sorts the outcomes into improvements, regressions, persistent failures, and stable successes, and writes longitudinal guidance into a protected region of the document that step-level edits are not allowed to overwrite. It is the head chef's weekly review, kept on a part of the card the line cooks cannot touch.

Alongside it sits a meta-skill memory: an optimizer-side summary of which kinds of edits have tended to work or fail, prepended to future reflection prompts. Crucially it lives with the optimizer, never in the shipped skill — so it costs nothing at deployment. In fact the whole apparatus adds zero inference-time model calls at deploy: the optimizer is a training-time tool, and what ships is just a short text file the agent reads.

# SkillOpt: gradient descent over a skill document
best ← s0; cur ← s0 # start from empty / seed skill
for epoch = 1 … E:
for each step t in epoch: # FAST path
traces ← run(cur, rollout batch) # frozen agent; score r
edits ← optimizer(success/failure traces, buffer) # textual gradient
edits ← rank(edits)[: L_t] # learning rate = edit budget
s˜ ← apply(cur, edits)
if r_sel(s˜) > r_sel(cur): # strict validation gate
cur ← s˜; if r_sel(s˜) > r_sel(best): best ← s˜
else: buffer ← buffer ∪ edits # momentum: remember dead ends
if epoch ≥ 2: # SLOW path
guidance ← reflect(start-of-epoch vs end-of-epoch skill)
cur ← write_protected(cur, guidance); gate as above
return best # best_skill.md — zero extra inference cost at deploy

What it actually buys you

The headline run: on GPT-5.5 in direct chat, adding a SkillOpt-trained skill lifts the six-benchmark average by +23.5 points. Inside agentic harnesses the gain holds or grows — +24.8 points average inside Codex, +19.1 inside Claude Code. Across all 6 benchmarks × 7 models × 3 harnesses, SkillOpt is best or tied-best on all 52 evaluated cells, beating every per-cell competitor including human-written skills, one-shot LLM skills, TextGrad, and GEPA.

Two details make the numbers more than a leaderboard. First, the winning skills stay tiny: 379–1,995 tokens after only 1–4 accepted edits — short enough to read, audit, and paste. Second, they transfer: an artifact optimized on one model retains value moved across model scales, between the Codex and Claude Code harnesses, and onto a nearby math benchmark, with no re-optimization. The disciplined loop produces a skill, not an overfit incantation.

Where it costs you, and where the analogy leaks

  • The “gradient” is a metaphor, not calculus. Nothing is differentiated. The gradient is an LLM's guess at a good edit, and the learning rate is a heuristic count of edits. The framing is genuinely productive — it imports schedules, momentum, and validation — but it is an analogy, and its guarantees are empirical, not mathematical.
  • It raises floors more than ceilings. The biggest jumps land where the base model was weakest (SpreadsheetBench 41.8, OfficeQA 33.1 gain roughly +39). On already-strong tasks (ALFWorld 83.6, DocVQA 78.8) the gain is a more modest +12. SkillOpt fixes what a model is bad at; it does not manufacture new peak ability.
  • “Zero inference cost” is at deployment only. Training spends many scored rollouts, a strong optimizer model, and repeated validation passes. The saving is that none of that rides along at serve time — but the optimization itself is not cheap.
  • It needs a scorable task. The loop descends a measured signal; where success cannot be scored by a harness, there is no loss to minimize and nothing for the gradient to point at.
  • Read the results as the authors' own suite. “Best-or-tied on 52/52” counts ties, on benchmarks and baselines the authors selected, in a 2026 paper this write-up has not independently reproduced. Strong, but not yet weathered by outside replication.

Where it sits

SkillOpt is one point on a fast-moving line. ProTeGi and TextGrad established the textual gradient; GEPA added reflection and a Pareto frontier over prompts; SkillOpt's bet is that the missing ingredient was not a cleverer gradient but optimizer hygiene — a controllable step size, a strict gate, momentum, a schedule — applied to a single persistent skill file. The result is an artifact you can read and move, produced by a loop that provably never regresses on its validation split.

The open edges are the interesting ones: the gate is strict and therefore conservative, so it can stall in noisy or plateaued regions; the “learning rate” is still a hand-set budget rather than something adapted from the loss surface; and the whole thing presumes a task you can score. But the reframing is the lasting part. Once a prompt is a parameter, everything we know about training parameters — schedules, momentum, early stopping, regularization — becomes fair game for text.
