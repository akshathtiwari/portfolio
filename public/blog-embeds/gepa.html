Reading the Film,
Not the Scoreboard

Reinforcement learning teaches a language model from a single number: the reward. GEPA argues that the words in a failed rollout carry far more signal than its score, and that an optimizer which reads the trace, writes a diagnosis, and edits the prompt can beat RL while spending up to 35× fewer rollouts.

35× fewer rollouts vs GRPO +6–20% over GRPO +>10% over MIPROv2 Reflective + Pareto

A deep dive on GEPA (Genetic-Pareto). Agrawal et al., 2025 — UC Berkeley, Stanford, Databricks, MIT and others.

The one idea

A scalar reward compresses an entire trajectory into a single number and throws the rest away. Language does not have to. GEPA lets a model read what went wrong in plain text and rewrite its own instructions, and it keeps a roster of prompts where each one is the best at something rather than chasing a single best-on-average winner.

The metaphor we will keep

Two coaching staffs prepare for the season. The first is told only the final score of each game and must infer, over thousands of games, which adjustments helped. The second watches the game film, writes notes on the exact play that broke, and rewrites that page of the playbook before the next game. GEPA is the second staff. The film is the execution trace, the score is the reward, the playbook is the prompt, and the depth chart of specialists is the Pareto frontier.

The signal problem

Adapting a language model to a new task usually means one of two things. You can change its weights with reinforcement learning, or you can change its text — the prompts and instructions that steer it. GEPA is squarely in the second camp, and to see why that camp matters you first have to feel how little information a reward carries.

The dominant RL recipe for LLMs is a policy-gradient method like GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization). You sample a trajectory τ\tauτ — a full run of the system, with its reasoning, tool calls and outputs — score it with a reward, and nudge the weights θ\thetaθ up the gradient of expected reward:

Here πθ\pi_\thetaπθ​ is the model, log⁡πθ(τ)\log \pi_\theta(\tau)logπθ​(τ) the log-probability it assigned to that trajectory, and A(τ)A(\tau)A(τ) the advantage — a scalar saying whether this run beat the group average. Stare at that A(τ)A(\tau)A(τ). Everything the run did, every reasoning step and tool result, is squeezed into one number before it can teach the model anything. That is the scoreboard. The play-by-play film is discarded.

Because each rollout returns so little, you need enormous numbers of them. In GEPA's own comparison, the GRPO baseline consumes roughly 24,000 rollouts (with LoRA) to learn a task. That is the pain the paper sets out to remove.

So the obvious question: if the trajectory is full of readable information, why are we throwing it away?

Two things you would try first — and how they break

Naive attempt 1: keep using the scalar

Push harder on RL. Collect more rollouts, tune the reward. This works, eventually, but the sample cost is the whole problem. A scalar advantage gives the model no direction in text space; it must rediscover, from the win/loss column alone, that (say) it keeps forgetting to cite a source or mis-formatting a tool call. Thousands of games to learn what one film session would show.

Naive attempt 2: let an LLM improve the prompt, greedily

Fine — use the words. Run the system, ask a strong model “here is what happened, write a better prompt,” keep the new prompt if it scores higher, repeat. This is the right instinct, and it is where GEPA starts. But the naive version has a specific, fatal move: always mutate the current best candidate.

Greedy best-average selection collapses. The paper reports that always picking the single best-scoring candidate drove the search into a local optimum after essentially one improvement, then spent the rest of the budget failing to refine it. In coaching terms: you crown one game plan, bench everything else, and now every practice rep tries to polish that one plan — including throwing away the trick play that was the only thing that beat the blitz.

Why this is the load-bearing failureThe reason GEPA is more than “ask GPT to fix the prompt in a loop” is almost entirely its answer to which candidate do I improve next? Hold onto the collapse above; the Pareto section is built to prevent exactly it.

GEPA keeps the reflective-rewrite instinct from attempt 2 and fixes its selection. Two pieces: how it mutates, and how it chooses what to mutate.

Piece one: reflective mutation — watch the film

Instead of collapsing a rollout to a reward, GEPA hands the whole trace to a reflector LLM: the reasoning, the tool calls, the tool outputs, and — crucially — the evaluation metric's textual feedback. Not “score 0.4,” but “the code failed to compile with this error,” “this unit test failed,” “the retrieved passage did not contain the answer.” The paper calls this rich diagnostic text Actionable Side Information: the text-optimization analogue of a gradient.

The reflector's output is not a vague “try harder.” In practice it produces structured rewrites — sections like Key Observations, Purpose and Context, Practical Strategy — that read like a coach's annotated page. The mutation is directed, not the random point-mutation of classical genetic algorithms. That is the “reflective” half of the name doing real work.

The new prompt is then tried on a small minibatch (size 3). If it improves, it joins the pool. Cheap to propose, cheap to test, and every proposal is anchored to a concrete failure it was written to fix.

Now the hard part. We have a growing pool of candidate prompts. Which one do we hand to the reflector next? Answer this wrong and you get the greedy collapse from before.

Piece two: the depth chart — Pareto selection

Here is GEPA's real idea. Do not track the best prompt. Track, for every single validation instance, which prompt is best on that instance — and keep all of them.

Let S[k][i]S[k][i]S[k][i] be the score of candidate kkk on validation instance iii. For each instance, find the best score anyone achieves, and collect every candidate that ties it:

s∗[i]  =  max⁡k S[k][i],P∗[i]  =  { k  :  S[k][i]=s∗[i] }s^{*}[i] \;=\; \max_{k}\, S[k][i], \qquad\qquad \mathcal{P}^{*}[i] \;=\; \big\{\, k \;:\; S[k][i] = s^{*}[i] \,\big\}s∗[i]=kmax​S[k][i],P∗[i]={k:S[k][i]=s∗[i]}

That is the instance-wise Pareto frontier: the set of candidates that are champion of at least one instance. A prompt with a mediocre average survives as long as it is the sole master of one hard case — the trick play that beats one specific defense. GEPA then prunes candidates that are strictly dominated (some other candidate matches or beats them on every instance and wins somewhere), and samples the next parent stochastically, weighted by how many instances each candidate wins:

Candidates that are best at more things are likelier to be chosen — so the search still exploits — but nothing that wins anywhere is ever cut, so the search never forgets an insight. One more genetic operator makes the roster more than the sum of its parts.

Piece three: system-aware merge

Real targets are compound systems — pipelines of several LLM modules, each with its own prompt. Over a run, different lineages improve different modules: one branch found a great retrieval instruction, another a great answer-formatting instruction. GEPA's system-aware merge is crossover with knowledge of that structure: take two Pareto-optimal candidates that excel on different modules and splice their improved prompts into one child that inherits both strengths.

In coaching terms, you combine one staff's passing game plan with another's running game plan. Because the merge is guided by which module each parent actually improved, it is far more likely to produce a viable child than blindly averaging two prompts.

The loop, assembled

Every piece is now motivated, so the algorithm is just their order. GEPA counts its budget in rollouts — system executions on task instances — and runs until the budget BBB is spent.

# GEPA: reflective prompt evolution with Pareto selection
init pool P ← { seed prompt }; score it on the Pareto validation set
while rollouts spent < B:
Φ ← select_parent(P) # Pareto sample: prob ∝ instances won
m ← choose_module(Φ) # which prompt in the system to edit
trace ← run(Φ, minibatch of 3) # capture reasoning, tools, textual feedback
edit ← reflect(trace) # LLM diagnoses in words, rewrites module m
Φ′ ← Φ with module m ← edit
if score(Φ′) > score(Φ) on minibatch:
evaluate Φ′ on Pareto set; add to P
optionally: Φ″ ← merge(two Pareto candidates); test & add
return best candidate on held-out data

Two design choices keep it honest. The reflector only ever sees the score of held-out validation instances, never their answers, which limits how much it can memorize. And accepting a child only when it improves keeps the pool monotone in quality while the Pareto sampler keeps it diverse.

What it actually buys you

The headline comparison is against GRPO. Across their benchmark suite, GEPA outperforms GRPO by 6% on average and by up to 20%, while using up to 35× fewer rollouts — turning a budget of tens of thousands of rollouts into a few hundred. Against MIPROv2, the leading prompt optimizer, GEPA wins by more than 10% (for example, +12% on AIME-2025), and does it with prompts about 33% shorter; GEPA's instruction-only optimization even beats MIPROv2's instruction-plus-few-shot optimization.

Where it costs you, and where it breaks

The same richness that makes GEPA strong is also its main hazard. Four honest caveats:

  • Prompt overfitting and bloat. Because the reflector reads real failures, it can bake specific training examples straight into the prompt, and detail accumulates across iterations. Left unchecked, prompts grow long and generalize worse. Practitioners counter this with a strict held-out validation set that returns only a score, and with length regularization or length-aware proposers (community reports of roughly 4× compression at little quality cost — a practitioner result, not the paper's).
  • The reflector must be strong. It is reasoning about reasoning; small models fail at it. GEPA effectively moves cost from many cheap rollouts to a few expensive frontier-model reflection calls. The saving is real, but it is not free.
  • Failure has to be sayable. GEPA needs a metric that emits text, not just a number — “feedback engineering.” In domains where the failure mode cannot be expressed in language (a subtle numeric-quality signal, say), there is little for the reflector to grip.
  • The data-abundant regime is unsettled. The authors are explicit that when rollouts and data are plentiful, full fine-tuning may still win. GEPA's decisive edge is the few-rollout regime.
On the code-optimization numbersGEPA also works as an inference-time search over code. On NPUEval (AMD XDNA2 kernels) it lifted GPT-4o from 4.25% to 30.52% mean vector utilization; on KernelBench it pushed a near-zero fast1 score above 20% with more search budget. The paper explicitly calls these early results that warrant further study, and this deep dive treats them the same way.

What comes next

GEPA ships as an open library and as an optimizer inside DSPy, where prompts are declared parameters and you swap optimizers the way you would swap a training loop. Its extension point is a small adapter with two methods: one that runs your system and returns a score, and one that surfaces the diagnostic trace for reflection — which is exactly the “feedback engineering” surface the caveats above point at.

The open questions are the interesting ones. How does reflection quality scale with the optimizer model? Can the loop extend past single-module edits to whole agent architectures? And the deepest one, the framing of the whole paper: reward is not the only teacher. Wherever a system can say why it failed, that sentence may be worth thousands of rollouts of a scalar trying to imply the same thing.
