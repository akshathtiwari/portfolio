Drafting the Tree You Verify

A speculative drafter proposes tokens a big model checks in parallel. Draft a single chain and one early wrong token throws away the rest of the block. TreeSpark drafts a tree of alternatives from one pass, and — the actual contribution — learns which branches to keep using target signals it already had, for no extra forward passes.

chain → trained tree +23–32% acceptance vs DSpark +15–25% throughput zero extra target passes

Zhao, Jiang, Zu et al., 2026. Extends DSpark.

Series: scaled attention → speculative decoding → DFlash → DSpark → TreeSpark

The one idea

Keep the alternatives you were throwing away. A single draft chain dies at its first mismatch; a tree of parent-conditioned alternatives, built from one backbone pass, survives it. The hard part is not building the tree — it is training it, and TreeSpark does that with two objectives that reuse the target distributions already computed along the reference chain, so the extra supervision costs zero extra target forward passes.

The metaphor we will keep

Think of a chess player preparing openings. Memorize a single line and the first time your opponent plays something off-script, you are on your own. Prepare a tree of replies — the likely moves at each junction — and a surprise still lands on a position you have studied. You can only hold so many positions in your head (a budget), so you prep the lines they are actually likely to play, and you make sure you have something ready for any dangerous sideline. Crucially, you prepare using the engine evaluation you already ran for the main line, not a fresh deep search for every branch.

The speedometer: acceptance length

Speculative decoding runs a small, fast drafter that proposes several tokens, and a large target model that verifies them in one parallel pass. Accepted tokens are committed; the first rejection ends the round with one free “bonus” token from the target. It is provably lossless — the output is identical to ordinary decoding — so the only thing that matters for speed is the acceptance length: how many tokens you commit per verification round.

The whole lineage is a fight to raise that number. DFlash drafts a whole block in parallel — fast, but blind, because no drafted token sees its neighbours, so acceptance decays down the block. DSpark fixes the blindness with a tiny semi-autoregressive head: one backbone pass, then a cheap per-depth correction so each token conditions on its parent. It drafts a coherent chain. TreeSpark asks the next question: why only one chain?

Because a chain has a brutal failure mode, and you have already felt it if you have ever prepared one chess line.

Why one wrong turn ends the block

Verification walks the draft left to right and stops at the first token the target would not have produced. So acceptance is a product of survivals: to commit token 5 you must have survived tokens 1 through 4. Even a well-trained chain drafter is only right about each position some of the time, and those probabilities multiply. In TreeSpark's own measurements, the DSpark chain is accepted 78.8% of the time at position 1 but only 24.7% by position 7 — so the deep tokens almost never survive, and the mean block barely reaches four.

A tree breaks the product. If, at each junction, you draft not just the single most likely next token but the top kkk alternatives, then a surprise no longer has to match your one guess — it only has to match one of kkk. The verifier follows whichever branch the target actually took. The same measurement shows the effect: position-1 acceptance rises to 90.0% and position-7 to 37.0%, and the mean block grows by roughly a quarter. So trees are better. But here is the catch that makes TreeSpark a paper and not a tweak: who taught the drafter what to put on those extra branches?

The untrained tree

Standard drafters are trained by reference-chain distillation: you run the target once, get the sequence of distributions it produces along the single reference path, and train the drafter to match them. That supervises exactly one chain of parent states — the reference one. The moment you keep alternatives, you are asking the drafter for distributions qθ(⋅∣u,x)q_\theta(\cdot \mid u, x)qθ​(⋅∣u,x) conditioned on parent nodes uuu it was never trained on, and to decide which sibling alternatives are worth keeping — a choice no reference chain ever expressed.

This is why bolting a token-tree onto an ordinary drafter underwhelms: the branches are untrained guesses. The retained alternatives are the ones the drafter happens to rank highly, not the ones the target would actually take. You pay for a wider tree and most of it is dead wood.

TreeSpark's contribution is two objectives that supervise the tree — both built entirely from target signals you already computed. First, how to build the tree at all.

One pass, many branches

TreeSpark inherits DSpark's trick: a single backbone pass produces a logit vector ℓd(x)\ell_d(x)ℓd​(x) for every draft depth d∈{1,…,B}d \in \{1,\dots,B\}d∈{1,…,B} (with B=7B=7B=7). To branch, it conditions each depth on the specific parent token tut_utu​ with a cheap additive correction (Eq. 1):

The point: a different parent gives a different distribution, but it costs only an extra head evaluation, never another backbone pass. Each frontier parent proposes its top kkk children (k=4k=4k=4), each child inherits a joint log-score (Eq. 5):

From the pool of up to k+(B−1)k2=100k + (B-1)k^2 = 100k+(B−1)k2=100 candidates, a global packing step keeps the N=32N=32N=32 highest-scoring nodes. Because log-probabilities are never positive, a parent always scores at least as high as any child, so the kept set is automatically a valid tree — every retained node has all its ancestors retained (the paper's “prefix closure”). That is the budget: 32 memorized positions, chosen by score.

Now the two objectives that make those 32 the right 32.

Objective one: survival NLL — weight what you keep

Not every branch deserves equal training weight. A branch that the target would follow with high probability matters far more than a deep, unlikely one. TreeSpark measures this with reference-derived path mass (Eq. 6): the product of the frozen target's probabilities along the path to a node,

m(ρ)=1,m(v)  =  m(pa(v))⋅pˉd(v)(tv)m(\rho) = 1, \qquad m(v) \;=\; m\big(\mathrm{pa}(v)\big)\cdot \bar{p}_{d(v)}(t_v)m(ρ)=1,m(v)=m(pa(v))⋅pˉ​d(v)​(tv​)

In the chess metaphor: spend your memorization on the lines the opponent is actually likely to play, in proportion to how likely the whole line is. But this term only pushes up tokens that are already in the tree. It says nothing about alternatives that got dropped — and some of those are exactly the ones the target will take.

That gap is what the second objective is for.

Objective two: the coverage hinge — rescue the right sidelines

Here is the subtle failure. The drafter keeps the top kkk tokens by its own logit. Suppose a token the target strongly favours sits just below that cutoff in the drafter's ranking. It gets dropped, it is never on a branch, and when the target takes it the round ends early. The survival term cannot help — the token is not in the tree to be weighted.

The capped coverage hinge (Eq. 3) supervises exactly these near-miss tokens. For a parent uuu at depth ddd, let bd(u)b_d(u)bd​(u) be the kkk-th largest draft logit — the local admission threshold — and penalize each target-favoured token iii that is not comfortably above it:

Both objectives read pˉd\bar{p}_dpˉ​d​ straight from the frozen target's output projection applied to features the training pass already cached. The same pˉd\bar{p}_dpˉ​d​ serves every parent at that depth. So the total loss adds two tree-aware terms on top of DSpark's base loss for free (Eq. 4):

The honest seamReusing pˉd\bar{p}_dpˉ​d​ on off-reference branches is an approximation: those target probabilities are exact only on the reference context, not on the alternative parents they are applied to. It is the chess player judging a sideline with the engine eval from the main line. The paper leans on it working well in practice; it does not bound the error.

Verifying a tree

At inference the packed tree is verified in one target pass using tree attention: each node attends only to the committed prefix plus its own ancestor path, and sits at position t+dt + dt+d for depth ddd. Greedy verification starts at the root and follows the child matching the target's argmax; if no child matches, it emits the target's argmax as a bonus token and ends the round. A completed round commits Ag+1A_g + 1Ag​+1 tokens, where AgA_gAg​ is the number of accepted non-root nodes. The output is provably identical to greedy autoregressive decoding.

# TreeSpark: one decode round
logits ← backbone(prefix) # ONE pass -> per-depth logits l_d
for depth d = 1 .. B: # expand
for each frontier parent u:
q_d(.|u) ← softmax(l_d + W·e(t_u)) # parent-conditioned, Eq.1
add top-k children by joint score s(v) # Eq.5
tree ← pack top-N nodes by score # N=32, prefix-closed
targets ← target(tree) # ONE verify pass, tree attention
walk root-down following target argmax; stop at first miss
commit A_g accepted nodes + 1 bonus token # lossless

The cost is real: a chain commits at most 8 tokens and feeds the verifier 7 positions, while TreeSpark feeds it up to 33. The tree buys acceptance with verifier width, which is why the throughput story depends on load.

What it actually buys you

On nine general benchmarks with greedy decoding, TreeSpark lifts mean acceptance length over DSpark from 4.23 to 5.25 on Qwen3-4B (+24.1%) and 4.20 to 5.18 on Qwen3-8B (+23.4%). On six writing domains the gain is larger — up to +31.6% — because open-ended text is where chains mismatch earliest. Single-request throughput rises +15.1% to +25.0%; batched Qwen3-4B throughput +12.6% to +17.2% depending on concurrency.

But read the ablation before you credit the objectives for all of it. The headline gain over DSpark bundles three changes at once: the tree policy, a wider attention head (dimension 192 vs 128), and the two new objectives. The paper's fixed-capacity control — same tree, same head, toggling only the supervision — isolates the objectives' true contribution at about +6.4% acceptance length and +4.2% to +5.0% system throughput. Dropping the survival term alone costs more than dropping coverage (system throughput falls from +17.2% to +11.9% without survival, versus +16.0% without coverage), so the mass-weighting is the heavier lifter of the two.

Where it costs you, and where it breaks

  • The clever part is a slice of the headline. Most of the +24% is the tree-versus-chain policy and the wider head; the novel supervision is worth a clean +6.4% acceptance on the controlled comparison. Real, but smaller than the top-line number suggests.
  • The free lunch is an approximation. Both objectives reuse reference-chain target probabilities on branches those probabilities were not computed for. It is exact only on the reference path; the paper demonstrates it empirically rather than bounding the error.
  • The tree is not free at serve time. Up to 33 verifier positions per round versus a chain's 7. Under heavy batching that extra width competes for the same capacity, which is why the batched gains (+12.6% at concurrency 36) are smaller than the single-request ones (+25%).
  • Narrow evaluation. Greedy decoding only, no stochastic sampling tested; a single fixed candidate budget; two Qwen3 sizes; results self-reported on Ascend 910B hardware with no error bars or significance tests, and summarizing per-request throughput rather than tail latency.

Where it sits

TreeSpark is the fifth step in a tight lineage. Speculative decoding made drafting worth doing; DFlash made it parallel; DSpark made the parallel draft coherent with a semi-autoregressive head; token-tree methods like EAGLE and Medusa showed verification could follow a tree. TreeSpark's addition is to take that tree seriously as something you train — supervising the parent-conditioned branches and the near-boundary alternatives with signals distillation already produces. The lasting idea is not the specific hinge; it is that the information to train a draft tree was sitting unused in the reference distributions all along.

The open edges: the reference-reuse approximation wants a real bound; the budget and branch width are fixed hyperparameters a load-aware scheduler could set the way DSpark scheduled verify length; and the whole thing is greedy-only so far. But as a way to spend the verifier width you were already paying for, drafting the tree you verify — and learning its branches for free — is a clean move.
