00 a faster way to run a slow model

Guess ahead.
Then verify in bulk.

Big language models write one word at a time, and each word is a full trip through billions of parameters. Speculative decoding is a trick to get several words per trip — without changing a single word of the output.

01 the bottleneck

Why writing one word at a time is so slow

A language model generates text autoregressively: it predicts the next word, appends it, then feeds the whole thing back in to predict the word after that. Word by word, in strict order — position 12 can't be computed until position 11 exists.

Here's the expensive part. Each of those predictions is one forward pass: the model reads its billions of parameters out of memory to produce a single next word. Writing a 200-word answer means doing that 200 times, in sequence. The clock time is basically "number of words × cost of one full pass."

And that cost is dominated by something surprising — not the arithmetic, but the memory traffic. To make even one word, the hardware has to haul the entire model's weights from memory to the chip. The actual multiply-adds are almost an afterthought; the chip spends most of its time waiting for weights to arrive.

Each word costs one full forward pass. Nine words → nine passes, strictly in order.

So the wall we're up against is: one full, memory-heavy pass per word, and the passes can't overlap. If we want to go faster, we somehow need to get more than one word out of a single pass. Which sounds impossible — until you notice one loophole.

02 the loophole

Checking eight words costs almost the same as making one

This single fact is the whole reason speculative decoding works.

Remember that a forward pass spends nearly all its time hauling weights from memory, not computing. Now here's the loophole: once the weights are on the chip, running them over eight words costs almost the same as running them over one. The expensive step — loading the model — already happened. Feeding a few more words through is nearly free.

Put differently: the model can read and score a whole span of words in parallel, in a single pass. It just can't invent them in parallel, because inventing word 12 requires already knowing word 11. Reading is parallel; writing is sequential.

03 the move

A fast junior drafts; a careful editor checks

Speculative decoding runs two models. The draft model is small and fast — think a keen junior who types quickly and is usually right. The target model is the big, authoritative one whose output we actually want — the careful editor.

The loop is:

1. Draft. The little model quickly writes the next few words on its own — say four. This is cheap because it's a small model, even though it's sequential.
2. Verify. The big model reads the draft's four guesses in a single parallel pass (that's the loophole from section 02) and, at every position, forms its own opinion of what the word should be.
3. Settle. Walk left to right. Wherever the editor agrees with the junior, keep that word. At the first disagreement, correct it to the editor's choice and throw away the rest of the draft (everything after a wrong word was written on a false premise).

The magic is in the accounting. If the junior got the first three words right, one editor-pass just produced four correct words (three kept + one correction). And if the junior nailed all four, the editor's parallel pass already computed the opinion for a fifth, bonus word too — so you bank five words for the price of one pass.

"Never worse, often better" only counts editor passes. The draft model does real work too, and those little passes aren't free — section 05 puts that cost back into the ledger and finds the break-even.

04 the rule that keeps it honest

Why the output is exactly what the big model would say

So far "the editor agrees" sounds fuzzy. If the editor just accepted whichever word it liked slightly, the final text would drift toward some blend of the two models — and you'd be quietly running a different, worse model. Speculative decoding avoids that with a precise acceptance rule that comes with a mathematical guarantee: the words you keep are distributed identically to sampling from the big model alone. Same quality, same randomness, same everything. The draft model only affects speed, never what gets said.

Here's the rule for a single position. The draft proposed a word with its own probability q. The big model assigns that same word probability p.

$$\text{accept the guess with probability } \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{p}{q}\right)$$

Read it plainly: if the big model likes the word at least as much as the draft did (p ≥ q), always keep it. If the big model likes it less (p < q), keep it only sometimes — precisely often enough to cancel out the draft's over-eagerness. And when a guess is rejected, you don't just grab the big model's favourite; you sample a replacement from the leftover distribution — the probability the big model still "owes" that the draft didn't already cover:

$$\text{resample from } \ \operatorname{norm}\big(\max(0,\ p - q)\big)$$

The one-line proof, if you want it

For any word x, it reaches the output through two doors. Door one: the draft proposes it (chance q(x)) and it's accepted (chance min(1, p(x)/q(x))). That product is simply min(q(x), p(x)). Door two: some guess is rejected and x is drawn from the leftover max(0, p−q). The total rejection mass exactly equals the leftover's mass, so door two contributes max(0, p(x) − q(x)).

$$\min(q,p) + \max(0,\,p-q) \;=\; p$$

The two doors sum to p(x) for every word. The output is distributed exactly as if the big model had sampled directly — no approximation, no drift. That equality is the entire reason anyone trusts this trick in production.

05 the ledger

How much faster, really?

The speed comes down to one number: the acceptance rate — how often the editor keeps a drafted word. Call it α. If the draft guesses γ words per round, the expected number of words banked per editor pass (including that free bonus word when a whole draft survives) is:

$$\text{words per pass} \;=\; \frac{1 - \alpha^{\,\gamma+1}}{1 - \alpha}$$

But drafting isn't free. Each of those γ little draft steps costs some fraction c of an editor pass (a good draft model is maybe 5–20× cheaper, so c ≈ 0.05–0.2). So the real speedup weighs words banked against total work done — editor pass plus the drafting:

$$\text{speedup} \;\approx\; \frac{\text{words per pass}}{1 + \gamma\, c}$$

Three knobs, one payoff. Find the sweet spot — and notice how over-guessing backfires when acceptance is low.

The pattern that falls out: when the draft is trustworthy (high α), guess boldly — long drafts pay off and the bonus word compounds. When it's shaky (low α), keep drafts short — every extra guess is likely wasted work that still costs c. And a cheaper draft model (small c) always shifts the sweet spot toward longer, braver speculation.

Real numbers are messier: acceptance isn't truly independent word-to-word, hardware batching changes c, and long drafts eventually hit compute limits that break the "verification is free" loophole. But the shape is right, and it's why production systems tune γ per workload rather than picking one value forever.

06 the tension

Picking a draft model is a tug-of-war

Everything hinges on the draft model, and it's pulled in two directions at once. Make it bigger and it guesses more like the target, so acceptance α climbs — but each draft step gets pricier, so c climbs too. Make it smaller and drafting is nearly free, but it guesses worse and gets corrected constantly. The whole art is landing in the middle:

too big a draft

High acceptance, but drafting is so expensive it eats the savings. In the extreme, your "draft" costs as much as the target and you've gained nothing.

too small a draft

Drafting is nearly free, but almost every guess is rejected. You're back to roughly one word per pass, plus wasted draft effort.

the sweet spot

A draft aligned to the target's style — often a small model from the same family, or the target distilled down — landing acceptance high enough that a short, cheap draft banks two-to-three words per pass.

Alignment matters as much as size. A draft trained on the same data, sharing the target's tokenizer and quirks, will agree far more often than a same-sized draft from a different lineage. Acceptance is about matching the target's taste, not raw capability.

07 the family tree

One idea, several disguises

"Guess ahead, verify in bulk, stay exact" is the shared skeleton. The variants mostly differ in where the guesses come from — because a separate draft model is the obvious source, but not the only one.

classic · separate draft model

The version we've walked through: a small standalone model proposes, the big one verifies. Simple, general, and the baseline everything else is measured against.

self-speculation · extra heads

Skip the second model entirely. Bolt a few lightweight "prediction heads" onto the target itself so it guesses several words ahead in one shot, then verifies them the same way. No separate model to host or keep aligned.

feature-level drafting

Instead of guessing raw words, predict the target's internal representations one step ahead and decode guesses from those. Cheaper, better-aligned guesses — higher acceptance from a very light drafter.

no draft at all · n-gram / lookahead

Draw candidate continuations from the text already generated or from simple n-gram patterns, then verify in parallel. Zero extra model — the guesses come from cheap heuristics, and the same exactness rule keeps the output honest.

They all inherit the same guarantee from section 04: because verification uses the accept/leftover rule, none of them change what the model says. They only change how cheaply the guesses are produced — and therefore how much speed you can steal from that memory-bound wall we started with.
