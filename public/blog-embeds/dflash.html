01 the cost nobody removed

Speculative decoding left one part slow

Quick recap. Speculative decoding pairs a small draft model that guesses the next few words with a big target model that verifies them all in one parallel pass, keeping the good guesses. A clever accept/reject rule makes the output identical to running the big model alone — same quality, just faster. (If that's new, it's worth reading about on its own first.)

But look closely at the drafting step. To guess γ words, the little model still runs γ times in a row — word 2 can't be drafted until word 1 exists. The drafter is small, but it's just as sequential as the model it's trying to accelerate. That's a real, unavoidable cost sitting inside every cycle:

$$T_{\text{draft}} \;=\; \gamma \cdot t_{\text{step}} \quad\text{(grows with every extra word you guess)}$$

Worse, there's a trap. To keep that sequential drafting cheap, the best methods make the draft model tiny — EAGLE-3, the state of the art, uses a single transformer layer. A one-layer model just isn't smart enough to guess many words correctly, so acceptance stalls. Guess more and drafting gets slow; shrink the drafter and it guesses badly. Autoregressive speculative decoding is boxed in around 2–3× speedup.

02 the two levers

Every speedup pulls on two ropes

There's a tidy way to see the whole game. The average time to produce one real word is the work done in a cycle divided by how many words that cycle banks:

$$L \;=\; \frac{T_{\text{draft}} + T_{\text{verify}}}{\tau}$$

Two levers, and only two. τ (tau) is how many words you accept per cycle — the bonus of good guessing. Push it up and each expensive verify-pass pays for more words. Tdraft is what the guessing costs. Push it down and every cycle gets cheaper. Lower the top, raise the bottom — either one speeds you up.

Here's the bind autoregressive drafting is in: the two levers are chained together. The only way to raise τ (better guesses) is a bigger draft model — but a bigger autoregressive drafter runs slower per step, which raises Tdraft. You pull one rope and the other tightens. EAGLE-3 lives at that knot: one tiny layer, because anything larger would cost too much to run sequentially.

DFlash's whole trick is to unchain the two levers — push τ up and Tdraft down at the same time. The next two sections are exactly those two moves.

03 move one · lower the drafting cost

Fill the whole block at once

This is where diffusion comes in — and where the sequential cost disappears.

A block diffusion model doesn't write left to right. It starts with a block of blank <mask> slots and fills them all simultaneously in one forward pass, the way an image sharpens from static all over at once rather than pixel by pixel. Guessing sixteen words costs one pass, not sixteen:

$$T_{\text{draft}}^{\text{diffusion}} \;=\; t_{\text{parallel}} \;\ll\; \gamma \cdot t_{\text{step}}$$

Because drafting a block costs the same whether it's shallow or deep, the draft model can afford to be bigger — DFlash uses five transformer layers where EAGLE-3 could only afford one. On a GPU, running five layers over a block in parallel is still faster than running one layer sixteen times in sequence. So you get a deeper, smarter drafter and lower latency. 04 move two · raise the acceptance

The target already knows what comes next

A parallel drafter is fast, but a fast drafter that guesses badly is worthless — rejected guesses are wasted work. So DFlash needs high acceptance too. Its insight is disarmingly simple: the target knows best.

When the big model reads your prompt, its internal layers don't just encode the next word — their hidden features quietly carry hints about the next several words. That information is already computed and just sitting there. So instead of asking a tiny drafter to figure out the future from scratch, DFlash hands it the target's own hidden features as a cheat sheet. The drafter stops being a separate little model and becomes a diffusion adapter riding on the target's understanding.

Features are pulled from several target layers (shallow → deep), fused, and injected into every draft layer.

But how you feed the drafter those features turns out to be everything. EAGLE-3 also uses target features — but only as an input at the very front of the draft model. Push that information through several draft layers and it fades, like a whisper passed down a line. So adding draft layers barely helps EAGLE — the guidance is gone by the time it reaches the deep layers.

DFlash injects the target's features directly into the Key and Value of every draft layer — storing them in the draft's KV cache so each layer reads them fresh. The guidance never fades. That single change is why DFlash's acceptance keeps climbing as you add draft layers, while input-only conditioning flattens out. Drag the depth slider and watch the two strategies diverge:

Those are honest, lossless numbers — the accept/reject rule guarantees the output matches plain Qwen3 exactly. But there's a caveat worth stating plainly, and the paper does: the "verification is nearly free" loophole holds when the GPU is memory-bound, which is the single-request regime. Pack many requests into one big batch and the hardware becomes compute-bound — now verifying a fat 16-word block for everyone actually costs something. Watch the speedup shrink as concurrency rises:

06 the craft

What makes the little drafter learn

A parallel, target-conditioned drafter is the architecture; getting it to actually train well took a handful of pointed choices. The most elegant is the loss weighting. In a block, an early mistake is catastrophic — if word 1 is wrong, verification stops there and words 2 through 16 are thrown away regardless of how good they were. So early positions deserve more attention during training:

$$w_k \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{k-1}{\gamma}\right)\quad\text{— weight for position } k \text{ in the block}$$

Earlier positions get a heavier weight, so the drafter learns to nail the front of each block first. Drag through the positions to see the emphasis fall off:

per-position training weight · block 16

Position 1 matters most because a miss there wastes the entire block. This alone measurably raised acceptance and sped up convergence.

random anchor blocks

At inference the drafter always starts a block right after a real word the target just produced. Training mirrors this: sprinkle "anchor" words through the text, start each block at an anchor, mask the rest. Randomizing anchors also multiplies the training signal from one sequence.

shared, frozen parts

The drafter borrows the target's token embedding and output head and freezes them — only its few transformer layers train. It stays a lightweight adapter, permanently aligned to the target's vocabulary and representation space.

train big, serve small

A drafter trained at block size 16 generalizes down to block size 8 at inference, but not the reverse. So you train once at a large block and shrink it on the fly under heavy load — free flexibility in production.

conditioning is the whole game

Strip out the target features and the exact same diffusion drafter drops from ~6× to ~2–3×. The parallel speed is nice, but the target's hidden features are what turn it into a genuinely good guesser.

07 the bigger idea

A new job for diffusion models

Diffusion language models have spent years trying to beat autoregressive models at their own game — generating finished, high-quality text end to end — and mostly losing, because good diffusion output needs many slow denoising steps. DFlash quietly changes the question.

As a drafter behind a lossless verifier, a diffusion model doesn't need to be good — it needs to be fast and roughly right. The target model catches every mistake, so you can slash the denoising all the way down to a single step and chase pure parallelism. Diffusion's biggest weakness — needing many steps for quality — simply stops mattering when something else guarantees the quality.

That's the reframe worth remembering: diffusion models may find their most practical home not as replacements for autoregressive models, but as their disposable, parallel scouts — running ahead, guessing in bulk, and letting the careful verifier decide what's true.

the problem

the equation
Speed depends on τ (words accepted per cycle) over T_draft + T_verify. Autoregressive drafting chains these together; you can't improve one without hurting the other.
move one
A block-diffusion drafter fills a whole block in one parallel pass, so drafting cost stops growing with block size — freeing room for a deeper (5-layer) drafter with no latency penalty.
move two
