Every layer edits a shared running document

A residual connection is one addition — x + F(x) — but it changed everything: it is why 100-layer networks train at all, why gradients survive the descent to the first layer, and the single object mechanistic interpretability is built on. This post derives all three from that one addition, then shows how Kimi K3 asks a sharp follow-up: if every layer reads a shared stream, why read it as a flat sum?

read  ~21 min math  gradient flow interactive  highways · depth-attention series  Attention · 6

Here is a paradox that stalled deep learning for years. A deeper network should never be worse than a shallower one: it could always set its extra layers to the identity function and exactly reproduce the shallow net. Yet in practice, stacking more layers made networks train worse — higher training error, not just worse generalization. He et al. (2015) named this the degradation problem. The network could not even learn to leave the extra layers alone. Something about optimizing very deep stacks was broken.

The one idea

A residual connection reframes a layer's job. Instead of computing a fresh representation H(x)H(x)H(x) from scratch, it computes a small edit F(x)F(x)F(x) to a shared running representation: x←x+F(x)x \leftarrow x + F(x)x←x+F(x). That one addition does three things at once — it makes very deep nets trainable (doing nothing means F→0F \to 0F→0, which is easy), it gives gradients a direct highway to every layer, and it turns the running xxx into the residual stream: a shared bus that every layer reads from and writes to, and the central object of interpretability.

We will take those three consequences in turn — trainability, gradient flow, interpretability — and then watch Kimi K3 push on the last assumption hiding in that innocent +++: that every layer's contribution is added with equal weight.

Residual learning: compute the edit, not the answer

The fix from ResNet is almost too small to believe. Take any block that would compute some function, and add its input back to its output:

H(x)=F(x)+x⟺F(x)=H(x)−x.H(x) = F(x) + x \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad F(x) = H(x) - x.H(x)=F(x)+x⟺F(x)=H(x)−x.

The block now only has to learn the residual F(x)=H(x)−xF(x) = H(x) - xF(x)=H(x)−x — the difference between what it wants to output and what it received. Why does this rescue the degradation problem? Because the hardest thing for a plain deep layer to learn turns out to be the identity: driving a stack of nonlinear transforms to reproduce their input exactly is a delicate balancing act. With a residual block, "do nothing" means simply F(x)=0F(x) = 0F(x)=0 — push the weights toward zero, which optimization does happily. As the paper puts it, residual networks are "easier to optimize." Suddenly 152 layers trained where a plain 30-layer net had choked.

"Easier to optimize" has a precise mechanism, and it lives in the backward pass.

The gradient highway, derived

and chain across all the layers from ℓ\ellℓ to LLL:

∂xL∂xℓ−1=∏i=ℓL(I+∂Fi∂xi−1).\frac{\partial x_L}{\partial x_{\ell-1}} = \prod_{i=\ell}^{L}\left(I + \frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_{i-1}}\right).∂xℓ−1​∂xL​​=i=ℓ∏L​(I+∂xi−1​∂Fi​​).

Now expand that product. One of its terms — the one that picks III from every factor — is exactly the identity III. So the gradient reaching layer ℓ\ellℓ always contains a clean, undamped copy of the gradient from the top:

∂L∂xℓ−1=∂L∂xL(I+(smaller terms)).\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x_{\ell-1}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x_L}\Big(I + (\text{smaller terms})\Big).∂xℓ−1​∂L​=∂xL​∂L​(I+(smaller terms)).

Contrast a plain network, xℓ=Fℓ(xℓ−1)x_\ell = F_\ell(x_{\ell-1})xℓ​=Fℓ​(xℓ−1​), whose gradient is a bare product ∏i∂Fi/∂xi−1\prod_i \partial F_i / \partial x_{i-1}∏i​∂Fi​/∂xi−1​ of Jacobians. If those have norm below 1 — the usual case — the product shrinks geometrically and the gradient vanishes exponentially with depth; above 1, it explodes. The residual's III term is a gradient highway: a route from the loss to every layer that is never multiplied down, so no layer is ever starved of signal, no matter how deep the stack. Identity initialization: start shallow, grow deep

There is a training-dynamics reading of the same fact. If each block is initialized so that Fℓ(x)≈0F_\ell(x) \approx 0Fℓ​(x)≈0 at the start (small final-layer weights, or an explicit learned scale set near zero), then xℓ≈xℓ−1x_\ell \approx x_{\ell-1}xℓ​≈xℓ−1​ and the whole deep network begins life as the identity — information passes straight through, untouched. Training then opts into depth gradually: each block learns a small, useful perturbation on top of the identity, growing the effective depth only as far as it helps. A residual network is, at initialization, a very shallow network wearing a deep coat, which is a much friendlier place to start gradient descent than a randomly-scrambled 100-layer transform.

The mental shift Without residuals, layer ℓ\ellℓ must reconstruct everything useful and forward it. With residuals, layer ℓ\ellℓ only has to decide what small thing to add. Representation becomes cumulative and incremental — which is exactly the picture the interpretability people took and ran with.

The residual stream: a shared bus layers read and write

Look at a transformer through this lens. Every sublayer — each attention block, each MLP — takes the form x←x+sublayer(x)x \leftarrow x + \text{sublayer}(x)x←x+sublayer(x). So there is a single vector xxx threading the entire depth of the model, and every component's only interaction with it is to read from it (project down into queries, keys, values, or MLP inputs) and write back to it (add its output). Elhage et al. (2021) named this thread the residual stream and made it the foundation of the transformer-circuits program.

Two properties make it the dominant mental model in mechanistic interpretability:

  • It is linear. The stream is only ever modified by addition. Nothing is multiplied into it or passed through a nonlinearity along the stream itself — the nonlinearities live inside the sublayers that read and write it. That linearity means contributions from different layers simply add, so you can decompose the final representation into a clean sum of "what each component wrote," and track a feature as a direction in the stream.
  • It is a communication channel. Because any layer can write a direction and any later layer can read it, heads in different layers compose by leaving messages for each other in the stream — exactly the induction-head circuit from the multi-head post, where a previous-token head writes and an induction head reads. The residual stream is the transformer's shared memory bus, and reading its traffic is how we reverse-engineer circuits.

But notice the assumption baked into "the stream is a sum": every layer's write sits in the stream with equal, permanent weight. Over great depth, that is a real limitation — and the frontier just attacked it.

Kimi K3's Attention Residuals: attention across depth

In a standard residual stream, the input that layer ℓ\ellℓ receives is the flat, equal-weighted sum of everything written before it:

xℓ−1=x0+∑i<ℓFi(xi−1)(every earlier write counted with weight 1).x_{\ell-1} = x_0 + \sum_{i<\ell} F_i(x_{i-1}) \quad\text{(every earlier write counted with weight 1)}.xℓ−1​=x0​+i<ℓ∑​Fi​(xi−1​)(every earlier write counted with weight 1).

As depth grows into the dozens or hundreds of layers, an individual early layer's contribution becomes a smaller and smaller fraction of that accumulated sum — diluted into an undifferentiated blend. A layer that would benefit from a specific earlier representation cannot selectively reach back and pull it out; it only sees the running total. Moonshot's Kimi K3 (an open 2.8-trillion-parameter model, 2026) addresses this with Attention Residuals (AttnRes), and the idea is beautifully recursive: use attention to read the residual history.

AttnRes in one line

Replace the fixed equal-weighted sum over previous layers with a softmax attention over previous layers' outputs. The current layer forms a query; the earlier layers' representations are the keys and values; each layer builds its residual input as a learned, input-dependent weighted combination of the depth history:

xℓ−1=Attn(qℓ; K<ℓ, V<ℓ),K<ℓ,V<ℓ={ outputs of layers 0,…,ℓ−1 }.x_{\ell-1} = \mathrm{Attn}\big(q_\ell;\ K_{<\ell},\ V_{<\ell}\big), \qquad K_{<\ell}, V_{<\ell} = \{\,\text{outputs of layers } 0,\dots,\ell-1\,\}.xℓ−1​=Attn(qℓ​; K<ℓ​, V<ℓ​),K<ℓ​,V<ℓ​={outputs of layers 0,…,ℓ−1}.

It is the exact same primitive this whole series has been about — a query, dot-product scores against keys, a softmax, a weighted average of values — but pointed at a new axis. Ordinary attention selects across tokens; AttnRes selects across layers. Where the standard residual stream forces all information through a uniform accumulation, AttnRes lets deep layers decide, per input, which earlier representations to route forward — recovering intermediate features that a flat sum would have buried.

Block AttnRes — keeping it affordable Attending over all previous layers is, unavoidably, quadratic in depth — O(L2d)O(L^2 d)O(L2d), the very same all-pairs cost from the last post, now on the layer axis instead of the token axis. So K3 uses Block AttnRes: partition the layers into a handful of blocks (on the order of eight) and attend over block-level representations rather than every individual layer. That drops the overhead to roughly linear in depth and bounds the extra state carried at inference, keeping most of the benefit at a small fraction of the cost.

What it costs and what's still open

  • AttnRes trades simplicity and cost for selectivity. The plain residual sum is free — one addition. Attention over depth adds parameters, compute, and (naively) an O(L2)O(L^2)O(L2) term; Block AttnRes buys most of the benefit back but is more machinery than a +++.
  • It complicates the clean residual-stream picture. The interpretability appeal of the standard stream is its linearity — contributions simply add. A learned softmax over layers makes the depth-mixing input-dependent and nonlinear, so the tidy "decompose the sum" story needs revisiting for AttnRes models.
  • The reported gains vary by source. Moonshot and secondary write-ups describe improved training efficiency and more uniform gradients across depth at a small overhead, but the exact figures differ across the materials available at launch (see the honesty note). Treat the mechanism as the solid takeaway and the specific percentages as provisional.
  • It is one point in a large design space. DenseNets, highway networks, and layer-wise gating explored non-uniform depth connectivity years earlier; AttnRes is a modern, attention-shaped instance of that long-running idea, not a bolt from the blue.

Where this leaves the series

Step back and the whole arc closes on a single primitive. Scaled dot-product attention mixes information across tokens; multi-head attention runs many such mixes in parallel; the quadratic cost of doing it all-pairs drives the efficiency literature; and now AttnRes applies the very same query–key–value mix across depth. Attention turned out to be less a single layer than a general operation — "form a query, score it against a set of keys, read a softmax-weighted average of values" — that the field keeps re-pointing at new axes: tokens, then heads, then layers.

The residual stream ties it together: it is the shared medium those token-mixing sublayers write into, the highway that makes the depth trainable, the canvas interpretability reads, and — with AttnRes — itself something to attend over. From tokens and embeddings to next-token prediction, that is the transformer, end to end.
