Compression & Information Theory

Distilled from 3Blue1Brown — "Reinventing Entropy" (Compression is Intelligence, Part 1). These notes follow the video's own arc and examples; formulas and numbers are the source's. A companion deep-dive I wrote is linked at the bottom.

TL;DR

Asking for the limit of text compression forces you to invent information theory. A perfect compressor's output looks like random noise, which means a message of probability p must cost −log₂ p bits — self-information. Averaging that over a distribution gives entropy H = −Σ p log₂ p, the provable floor on compression (Shannon's noiseless coding theorem). Because prediction and compression are equivalent, a language model trained with cross-entropy loss is literally learning to compress — and Shannon estimated English at ~1 bit/character.

📝The notes

1 · The question, and why it's deep

Encoding text as few bits as possible raises a natural question: is there a fundamental limit to compression? ASCII spends a flat 8 bits per character; a little cleverness (short codes for common characters) gets you to about 4 bits/char; leveraging longer patterns does better still. The single number matters less than the method for finding it — a method Shannon invented in the 1940s that turns out to underpin modern machine learning.

🔑 keepThe interesting object is not "the limit" but the reasoning that leads to it — that reasoning is information theory.

2 · The moon-rover warm-up

A rover receives one of four instructions with a non-uniform, independent distribution:

P(↑)=12,P(↓)=14,P(←)=18,P(→)=18.P(\uparrow)=\tfrac12,\quad P(\downarrow)=\tfrac14,\quad P(\leftarrow)=\tfrac18,\quad P(\rightarrow)=\tfrac18.P(↑)=21​,P(↓)=41​,P(←)=81​,P(→)=81​.

The warm-up puzzle: what is the most efficient way to encode this as a bitstream? It contains every essential idea needed for the general theory.

3 · Three students, three answers

  • Straightforward: two bits each — 00, 01, 10, 11. Easy to decode (chop into pairs) but ignores that ↑ is far more common. Average: 2 bits/instruction.
  • Clever: a variable-length code — 0 for ↑, 10 for ↓, 110 for ←, 111 for →. Weighted average: 12(1)+14(2)+18(3)+18(3)=1.75\tfrac12(1)+\tfrac14(2)+\tfrac18(3)+\tfrac18(3)=\mathbf{1.75}21​(1)+41​(2)+81​(3)+81​(3)=1.75 bits — better, by spending fewer bits on the frequent symbol.
  • Theoretical (head in the clouds): doesn't build a code at all; argues that perfect compression must look like random noise, and reasons from there.

4 · Prefix-free codes and the budget

For a variable-length code to be decodable, no codeword may be a prefix of another (a "prefix-free" or "prefix" code); then the decoder just reads bits until a complete codeword forms. Picture the binary tree of all bit strings: a codeword of length ℓ\ellℓ claims everything above it, which is a fraction 2−ℓ2^{-\ell}2−ℓ of the whole code space. In the clever code those fractions are 12,14,18,18\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac1821​,41​,81​,81​ — exactly the probabilities, and they fill the space with nothing left over.

🔑 keepA length-ℓ codeword costs 2−ℓ of the budget; the budget of all codewords sums to ≤ 1. Short codes are expensive.

5 · Why perfect compression = random noise

From the receiver's side: a compressed message of nnn bits is one of 2n2^n2n possible strings of that length. If the output is truly optimal it looks like random noise — all 2n2^n2n strings equally likely — which forces the underlying messages to each have probability 12n\tfrac{1}{2^n}2n1​. Try to make one message shorter and it collides on the tree, bumping two others up a level: "push down a bump in the rug and it pops up worse elsewhere." So for equally-likely messages, equal-length codewords are optimal, and a message of probability p=2−np=2^{-n}p=2−n must use nnn bits.

6 · Self-information: −log₂ p

Take p=2−np=2^{-n}p=2−n, take log⁡2\log_2log2​ of both sides, and negate: the bits for a message of probability ppp is

information(p)  =  −log⁡2p  =  log⁡21p.\text{information}(p) \;=\; -\log_2 p \;=\; \log_2\tfrac{1}{p}.information(p)=−log2​p=log2​p1​.

This is the founding formula of information theory. Read it as "how many times do you halve the space of possibilities to single out this event." It stays meaningful (as a lower bound, on average) even when ppp is not a clean power of two, giving fractional bits. Because independent probabilities multiply and log⁡\loglog turns products into sums, the information of a whole message is the sum of its symbols' information (the chain rule, logged):

−log⁡2P(x1…xT)  =  ∑t−log⁡2P(xt∣x<t).-\log_2 P(x_1\dots x_T) \;=\; \sum_{t} -\log_2 P(x_t\mid x_{<t}).−log2​P(x1​…xT​)=t∑​−log2​P(xt​∣x<t​).
🔑 keepRare = informative = expensive to encode. Certain events (p=1) cost 0 bits; a coin flip costs 1; one-in-1024 costs 10.

7 · Entropy: the average, and the floor

The average information per symbol of a distribution is Shannon entropy:

H  =  ∑ipi (−log⁡2pi)  =  −∑ipilog⁡2pibits/symbol.H \;=\; \sum_i p_i\,(-\log_2 p_i) \;=\; -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i \quad\text{bits/symbol.}H=i∑​pi​(−log2​pi​)=−i∑​pi​log2​pi​bits/symbol.

Visualise it as bars of width pip_ipi​ topped by rectangles of height −log⁡2pi-\log_2 p_i−log2​pi​; HHH is their total area. Shannon's noiseless coding theorem (1948) makes it exact: no lossless code beats HHH bits/symbol on average, and you can always get arbitrarily close. For the rover, H=1.75H=1.75H=1.75 — the clever code was provably optimal. Entropy is highest for an even distribution (maximum uncertainty, nothing to exploit) and drops toward 0 as one symbol dominates. (von Neumann reputedly suggested the name "entropy" — the story is likely apocryphal.)

🔑 keepEntropy = minimum average bits per symbol = the compression floor. Predictability is compressibility.

8 · How do you know the probabilities? (Shannon's methods)

For real language, probabilities are context-dependent and never clean powers of two, and there is no formula for "the true distribution of English." Shannon's approaches:

  • n-grams: scan books, record what follows short strings (e.g. what comes after "th"). Breaks down for long contexts — exactly where text is most predictable and most compressible.
  • The guessing game: he had his wife Betty guess each next letter; correct guesses became dashes, wrong ones the true letter. The reduced text carries the same information (a copy of Betty could regenerate the original).
  • The 1951 paper "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English": record how many guesses each next letter takes, map that to an implicit probability. With ≥100 characters of context he estimated English at ~1 bit/character.

The deep point: to estimate compressibility he had to probe an intelligent model of language — a human brain as a black box. Today we build those black boxes instead of interviewing them.

9 · The AI connection

Large language models are pre-trained on next-token prediction via cross-entropy loss — a term rooted in information theory. And a central result says prediction and compression are mathematically equivalent, two sides of one coin. So pre-training can be reframed: not "predict the next token" but "build the most efficient possible text compressor." Each per-token loss term −log⁡2q(xt∣x<t)-\log_2 q(x_t\mid x_{<t})−log2​q(xt​∣x<t​) is exactly the bits it would take to encode that token — so minimising the loss is minimising the compressed size.

compression is intelligence — hard to judge rigorously (intelligence is ill-defined), but the safer, defensible claim is that the mathematics of compression is bizarrely relevant to AI. Parts 2 & 3 of the series cover cross-entropy in training and an actual LLM-based compressor.

A · Motivation & the big picture

Why is asking for the limit of text compression a deep question rather than an engineering one?

Because you can't answer it without a theory of how much information text carries. Pinning down the limit forces you to invent self-information and entropy — the reasoning matters more than any single number.

State the equivalence at the heart of the "compression is intelligence" idea.

Prediction and compression are mathematically equivalent — two sides of one coin. A model that predicts the next symbol well can compress well, and vice versa.

What is the defensible version of "compression is intelligence"?

That the mathematics of compression is bizarrely relevant to AI. The stronger slogan is hard to judge because "intelligence" is ill-defined.

B · The rover & codes

The rover's four instructions have what probabilities, and why does that matter?

½, ¼, ⅛, ⅛ for up/down/left/right (independent). Because they're non-uniform, a fixed-length code wastes bits on the common symbol.

Give the clever variable-length code and its average bits per instruction.

0=↑, 10=↓, 110=←, 111=→. Average =12(1)+14(2)+18(3)+18(3)=1.75=\tfrac12(1)+\tfrac14(2)+\tfrac18(3)+\tfrac18(3)=\mathbf{1.75}=21​(1)+41​(2)+81​(3)+81​(3)=1.75 bits, vs 2 for the naive code.

What were the three students' strategies?

Straightforward: fixed 2 bits each. Clever: variable-length prefix code (1.75 bits). Theoretical: reason about what a perfect code must look like (random noise).

C · Prefix-free codes & the budget

What is the defining rule of a prefix-free (prefix) code, and why is it needed?

No codeword is a prefix of another. It lets the decoder read bits until a complete codeword forms, with no ambiguity about where instructions divide.

How much of the "code space" does a codeword of length ℓ consume?

A fraction 2−ℓ2^{-\ell}2−ℓ. On the binary tree it claims everything above it. All codewords' fractions must sum to at most 1.

What "coincidence" hints that the clever code is optimal?

The code-space fractions 12,14,18,18\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac1821​,41​,81​,81​ are exactly the probabilities, and they fill the budget with nothing left over.

Explain the "bump in the rug" argument for why equal-length codes are optimal for equally-likely messages.

Making one message's codeword shorter moves it down the tree, colliding with others and forcing them up a level (more bits). Saving one bit costs two elsewhere — so you can't beat equal lengths.

D · Self-information (−log₂ p)

Derive why a message of probability p costs −log₂ p bits.

Perfect compression looks like random noise, so a message using nnn bits has probability 2−n2^{-n}2−n. Set p=2−np=2^{-n}p=2−n, take log⁡2\log_2log2​, negate: n=−log⁡2pn=-\log_2 pn=−log2​p.

What's the intuitive reading of −log₂ p?

"How many times you halve the space of possibilities to single out an event of probability ppp." Equivalently log⁡2(1/p)\log_2(1/p)log2​(1/p).

How many bits do events of p = 1, ½, and 1/1024 carry?

0 bits, 1 bit, and 10 bits. Certain events are free; rarer events carry more information.

Why does the information of a whole message equal the sum of its symbols' information?

Independent (or chained) probabilities multiply, and log⁡\loglog turns products into sums: −log⁡2∏tP(xt)=∑t−log⁡2P(xt)-\log_2\prod_t P(x_t)=\sum_t -\log_2 P(x_t)−log2​∏t​P(xt​)=∑t​−log2​P(xt​).

Is −log₂ p an exact code length? What is it really?

Not per symbol (you can't emit fractional bits with fixed codewords). It's a lower bound on compression, averaged over messages; a real algorithm (Part 3) gets within 1–2 bits of the summed value for a whole message.

E · Entropy

Write the entropy formula and say what it measures.

H=−∑ipilog⁡2piH=-\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_iH=−∑i​pi​log2​pi​ = the average information per symbol = the minimum average bits/symbol to encode the source (compression floor).

What does the noiseless coding theorem (1948) state?

No lossless code can do better than HHH bits/symbol on average, and codes exist that get arbitrarily close to HHH.

When is entropy high vs. low?

High for an even (uniform) distribution — maximum uncertainty, little to exploit. Low when one symbol dominates. Spreading over more symbols also raises it.

How is entropy visualised as an area?

Stacked bars of width pip_ipi​ topped by rectangles of height −log⁡2pi-\log_2 p_i−log2​pi​; entropy is the total area (a probability-weighted sum of surprisals).

Why does the rover example confirm entropy is the floor?

Its entropy is exactly 1.75 bits, which the clever prefix code achieves — a perfect code where each codeword's length equals the symbol's information.

F · Estimating language (Shannon's methods)

Why do n-gram statistics fail for estimating language entropy?

Long strings mostly never appear in any corpus, yet long context is exactly where text is most predictable (and most compressible). Raw counts run out.

Describe Shannon's guessing game with Betty.

She guessed each next letter; correct guesses were written as dashes, wrong ones as the true letter. The reduced text carries the same information — a copy of the guesser could regenerate the original.

What did the 1951 paper measure, and what was the estimate?

"Prediction and Entropy of Printed English": how many guesses each letter needs → an implicit probability. With ≥100 characters of context, entropy ≈ 1 bit/character.

What's the conceptual punchline of Shannon's method?

To estimate compressibility he had to probe an intelligent model of language (a human brain as a black box). Modern ML designs those black boxes instead.

G · The AI connection

How is an LLM's cross-entropy pre-training loss a compression objective?

Each term −log⁡2q(xt∣x<t)-\log_2 q(x_t\mid x_{<t})−log2​q(xt​∣x<t​) is the bits to encode that token under the model. Summing = the compressed size; minimising the loss = minimising the file. Predicting well = compressing well.

Reframe LLM pre-training in one sentence using compression.

It is training the model to be the most efficient possible text compressor, rather than "just" next-token prediction.

What surprising fact does ~1 bit/char imply about English?

English could in principle be compressed to about one yes/no decision per character — most characters are highly predictable from context.

H · Interleaved mix

A symbol has p = 1/8. Optimal codeword length, and why?

3 bits, because −log⁡2(1/8)=3-\log_2(1/8)=3−log2​(1/8)=3; and a length-3 codeword consumes 2−3=1/82^{-3}=1/82−3=1/8 of the code budget — matching its probability.

Which is more compressible: a fair 4-sided die, or one that lands "1" 97% of the time? Why?

The biased one. Low entropy (one dominant outcome) means high predictability, so short codes for the common outcome win. The fair die is at maximum entropy (2 bits/symbol).

Connect: Betty's guessing game and an LLM computing cross-entropy loss.

Both read off how many bits a next symbol costs by querying a predictive model of language. Betty was a human black box; the LLM is an engineered one — same methodology, 70 years apart.

Why can't you just make every message one bit shorter to beat entropy?

Codewords share a finite budget on the binary tree; shortening one forces others longer (the "bump in the rug"). Averaged over messages you can't go below HHH — the noiseless coding theorem.

Give the chain from "perfect compression" to "−log₂ p" in three steps.

(1) Optimal output = random noise → each nnn-bit message equally likely with prob 2−n2^{-n}2−n. (2) So bits used =n=n=n where p=2−np=2^{-n}p=2−n. (3) Solve: bits =−log⁡2p=-\log_2 p=−log2​p.

⚠️Gotchas

You might think: −log₂ p gives each symbol a literal fractional-bit codeword. Actually: it's a bound on the average; real codes round to whole bits, and only clever schemes (arithmetic coding, Part 3) approach the summed value over a whole message.
You might think: the entropy formula H = −Σ p log p applies to language directly. Actually: it assumes every symbol follows the same distribution. Language needs the more general "entropy rate," estimated (not computed) — hence Shannon's experiments.
You might think: the probabilities from a language model are "the true probabilities of language." Actually: they're a model's estimate, and it's not even clear what "true probabilities of language" would mean — a caveat the video flags and Part 2 revisits.
Source-vs-fact note: the video dates "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English" to 1950; it was published in the Bell System Technical Journal in 1951. The noiseless coding theorem is from Shannon's 1948 founding paper.
